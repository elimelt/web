Quantization of a neural network replaces the number format in which its weights, and optionally its activations, were trained with a format of fewer bits. Each value is mapped to a point on a finite grid, and one or more scale factors map the grid points back to approximate real values.

Examples are 8-bit integers or 8-bit floats in place of 16-bit floats, and 4-bit codes for the weights. Quantization reduces serving cost in three ways. The model occupies less memory, so it fits on fewer GPUs or leaves more memory for the KV cache. Each decoded token reads fewer bytes, and at small batch sizes weight reads account for most of the time of a decode step. When the activations are quantized as well, the matrix multiplies run on low-precision tensor cores with higher throughput.

The main obstacle is the distribution of a trained network's activations. A small number of channels, and of tokens, carry values hundreds or thousands of times larger than the rest, and a single shared scale cannot represent both ranges accurately. Most of the [methods](#methods) are designed around these outliers.

The measurements in [Measured on T5](#measured-on-t5) use Google's released [T5 v1.1](https://github.com/google-research/text-to-text-transfer-transformer/blob/main/released_checkpoints.md) checkpoints in the LM-adapted variant, which continued training for 100K steps on a plain language-modeling objective, so they score continuations of text the way a decoder LLM does. All experiments run in numpy on a CPU with simulated quantization, in which each weight or activation is rounded to the low-precision grid and multiplied in float32. Simulation reproduces the accuracy of a real kernel and gives no information about its speed.

The material began as notes for CSE 599K "LLM Serving Systems" at the University of Washington, Spring 2025, taught by Prof. Baris Kasikci with TA Kan Zhu. Serving cost is modeled in [[ml/serving-systems/performance-modeling|Performance Modeling]] and [[ml/serving-systems/memory-management|Memory Management]].

## Effect on serving cost

Decoding one token streams every weight from memory and performs about two FLOPs per parameter. In 16 bits that is one FLOP per byte, far below the H100's ridge point of about 295 FLOPs per byte, the arithmetic intensity at which its tensor cores become the bottleneck ([[ml/serving-systems/performance-modeling#h100-numbers|Performance Modeling]]). At small batch sizes the time of a decode step is therefore approximately the weight bytes divided by memory bandwidth, and 4-bit weights reduce it by nearly 4x. The [Marlin](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11743) INT4-weight kernel reports close to the full 4x for batches up to 16 to 32 tokens, with the speedup declining as the multiply becomes compute bound. Beyond that point the weight bytes no longer determine step time, and further gains come from higher-throughput arithmetic. FP8 and INT8 tensor cores have about twice the dense throughput of BF16 on Hopper ([[ml/serving-systems/gpu-basics#numeric-format-throughput-tradeoffs|GPU Basics]]). vLLM's documentation reports [2x less model memory and up to 1.6x more throughput](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/stable/features/quantization/llm_compressor/fp8/) for FP8 weights and activations.

Memory capacity is the second effect. DeepSeek-V3 has 671B parameters. In BF16 they occupy about 1.34 TB, more than the 1.13 TB of HBM on an eight-GPU H200 node. DeepSeek released the weights only in FP8 E4M3, [about 689 GB](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3), which fits on one node and leaves memory for the KV cache. The KV cache is itself a quantization target, discussed under [The KV cache](#the-kv-cache).

Energy consumption follows the same pattern. In Horowitz's 45 nm figures, as [tabulated by Han et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02626), an 8-bit integer add costs 0.03 pJ and an 8-bit multiply 0.2 pJ, a 32-bit integer multiply 3.1 pJ and an FP32 multiply 3.7 pJ, while reading 32 bits from off-chip DRAM costs about 640 pJ. Multiplier energy grows roughly with the square of the bit width, and moving a number from DRAM consumes more energy than any arithmetic on it, so reducing the bytes moved saves more energy than reducing the precision of the arithmetic.

## Number formats

A floating-point format divides its bits between exponent and mantissa. The exponent bits determine range (how far apart the smallest and largest values are), and the mantissa bits determine precision (how close neighboring values sit). An integer format has a single fixed step, so its precision is uniform and its range is set by the scale factor.

| Format | Sign/exponent/mantissa bits | Largest finite value | Where it's used |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| FP32 | 1/8/23 | $3.4 \times 10^{38}$ | accumulation, reference |
| BF16 | 1/8/7 | $3.4 \times 10^{38}$ | training and serving default |
| FP16 | 1/5/10 | 65,504 | older serving default |
| FP8 E4M3 | 1/4/3 | 448 | weights and activations |
| FP8 E5M2 | 1/5/2 | 57,344 | gradients |
| INT8 | integer | 127 steps each side | weights and activations, with a scale |
| FP4 E2M1 | 1/2/1 | 6 | 4-bit weights and activations, block-scaled |
| INT4 | integer | 7 steps each side | weights, with a scale per group |

The FP8 definitions are from [Micikevicius et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05433), who recommend E4M3 for weights and activations and E5M2 for gradients. E4M3 omits infinities, which raises its largest finite value from 240 to 448. Which formats are practical depends on hardware support. FP8 tensor cores arrived with Hopper and Ada, and Blackwell adds 4-bit floating point ([Transformer Engine docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/transformer-engine/user-guide/index.html)).

BF16 retained FP32's exponent width so that training would not overflow, and the T5 measurements show that the same property matters for serving. T5 v1.1's residual stream reaches values near 500,000 on ordinary blog text (488,941 in the base model), far beyond FP16's maximum of 65,504, so the model cannot run in FP16 without clamping. Hugging Face's T5 code clamps infinities in FP16 and keeps the feed-forward output projection in FP32 when loading 8-bit weights. This is a workaround for [degenerate Flan-T5-XXL output in 8 bits](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/issues/20287), caused by overflow in that layer.

### Block-scaled formats

The newer 4- and 8-bit formats attach a scale to every small block of values instead of to a whole tensor or channel. The [OCP Microscaling (MX) spec](https://www.opencompute.org/documents/ocp-microscaling-formats-mx-v1-0-spec-final-pdf) defines MXFP8, MXFP6, MXFP4 and MXINT8, each with blocks of 32 elements sharing an 8-bit power-of-two scale (E8M0). NVIDIA's [NVFP4](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/introducing-nvfp4-for-efficient-and-accurate-low-precision-inference) uses blocks of 16 FP4 values with an FP8 E4M3 scale, which need not be a power of two, plus one FP32 scale per tensor, about 4.5 bits per value in all. Small blocks limit the effect of an outlier to the 16 or 32 values that share its scale.

## Linear quantization

The affine map between a real value $r$ and a $b$-bit integer $q$, with scale $S$ and zero point $Z$, is

$$q = \text{clip}\left(\text{round}\left(\frac{r}{S}\right) + Z,\; q_{min},\; q_{max}\right), \qquad r \approx S(q - Z)$$

Min-max calibration sets $S = (r_{max} - r_{min})/(q_{max} - q_{min})$. The error has two sources, rounding error of at most $S/2$ in either direction and clipping error for values outside the range. Choosing the range trades one against the other, since a wider range clips less and makes every step coarser.

Symmetric quantization fixes $Z = 0$ and $S = \max|r| / (2^{b-1} - 1)$, so INT8 uses the codes $-127$ to $127$. Its advantage follows from the expansion of a quantized matrix product,

$$Y = S_W S_X \left[q_W q_X - q_W Z_X - q_X Z_W + Z_W Z_X\right]$$

With both zero points zero, the three correction terms vanish and the kernel reduces to a plain integer matmul. Asymmetric quantization represents skewed distributions such as the output of a ReLU more accurately, at the cost of computing those terms. Weight-only kernels can use asymmetric INT4 without that cost because they dequantize the weights to 16 bits before multiplying.

Granularity is the second choice. A scale can apply to a whole tensor, an output channel, a token (for activations), or a group of $g$ consecutive weights. Finer granularity fits the data more closely and stores more scales. A group of 128 INT4 weights with a 16-bit scale and a 16-bit zero point occupies $4 + 32/128 = 4.25$ bits per weight. [Dettmers and Zettlemoyer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09720) ran more than 35,000 zero-shot experiments across model families and found that 4 bits per weight almost universally gives the highest accuracy for a given total number of model bits, with block sizes of 64 to 128.

> [!note]- Deep dive: non-linear quantization, codebooks and NF4
> When values are far from uniformly distributed, a uniform grid spends many codes on ranges that few values occupy. [Deep Compression](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.00149) clusters each layer's weights with k-means and stores a small full-precision codebook plus a $\log_2 k$-bit index per weight. Its compression ratio for $n$ weights of $b$ bits and $k$ clusters is $nb / (n \log_2 k + kb)$; the paper's toy example, 16 weights and 4 clusters, gives 3.2x. On AlexNet it used 8-bit indices for convolutions and 5-bit for fully connected layers with no accuracy loss, and with pruning and Huffman coding the whole pipeline shrank AlexNet 35x and VGG-16 49x. [QLoRA's NF4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314) is a fixed codebook instead, with 16 values at the quantiles of a standard normal, scaled per block of 64 weights, on the grounds that trained weights are roughly normal. Its block scales are themselves quantized to FP8, which reduces their overhead from 0.5 to about 0.127 bits per weight ([[ml/serving-systems/peft-and-preference-optimization#qlora-quantized-frozen-base|PEFT and Preference Optimization]]). Codebook formats require a table lookup in the kernel, whereas GPTQ and AWQ groups, FP8, MX and NVFP4 all use integer or floating-point grids.

## PTQ and QAT

Post-training quantization (PTQ) quantizes a trained model, possibly using a few hundred sequences of calibration text to choose scales. All measurements in [Measured on T5](#measured-on-t5) are PTQ. Quantization-aware training (QAT) simulates the rounding in the forward pass during training, so that the network learns weights robust to it. Rounding has zero gradient almost everywhere, so the backward pass uses the straight-through estimator, treating $\partial\, \text{round}(x) / \partial x$ as 1. At the same bit width QAT generally reaches higher accuracy than PTQ, and it requires a training run.

The choice of rounding direction can change accuracy by tens of percentage points. [AdaRound](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.10568) quantized only the first layer of a ResNet-18 to 4 bits and compared rounding rules on ImageNet. Round-to-nearest gave 52.29% accuracy, 100 draws of stochastic rounding gave 52.06 ± 5.52% with the best draw at 63.06%, and always rounding up or always down gave 0.10%. Round-to-nearest is not optimal per weight, because errors in different weights interact through the next layer. [GPTQ](#gptq) exploits the same interaction.

## Outliers

Quantization recipes that preserved accuracy on CNNs and small transformers lost accuracy on large language models. [Dettmers et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07339) attributed the degradation to outlier features. Around 6.7B parameters a phase shift occurs, after which a few hidden dimensions (about 0.1% of them, six dimensions at 6.7B) carry large values in every layer and at about 75% of sequence positions. A single scale per tensor sized for those values leaves too few steps for all other values.

Later work identified two related phenomena. [Sun et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17762) describe massive activations, a few values up to 100,000 times larger than the typical one, which occur at particular tokens (the first token, delimiters) and function as fixed biases in the model's computation. [Bondarenko et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12929) attribute strong outliers to attention heads that learn a no-op. To place almost all of its attention on an uninformative token, a head drives some values to very large magnitudes. They showed that a clipped softmax or gated attention removes the outliers and allows activations to be quantized to INT8 directly. Outliers also occur in smaller models. An earlier study by the same group found [BERT-base](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.12948) falling from 83.06 to 71.03 on GLUE with 8-bit weights and activations, while 8-bit weights alone scored 83.23, and traced the degradation to the residual sum after the feed-forward block.

### Outliers in T5

I recorded, for every linear layer in the three smallest LM-adapted T5 v1.1 models, the largest input magnitude in each channel over 64 calibration windows of Go blog text. The pattern is the same at all three sizes. The input to each feed-forward block's output projection holds values in the tens of thousands, far above the inputs to any other layer, and the ratio between its largest and median channels grows with model size.

| Model | Parameters | Residual stream max | Max input after a norm | Max input to `wo` | `wo`: top channel / median channel |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| small | 77M | 330,495 | 9.3 | 12,378 | 1,359× |
| base | 248M | 488,941 | 13.1 | 14,273 | 1,687× |
| large | 783M | 502,948 | 39.8 | 15,666 | 5,452× |

The inputs to the attention projections and to the first feed-forward layer follow an RMS norm and have a narrow range. Their largest channel is at most 44 times the median one, and the inputs to the attention output projection are within a factor of 8. The input to the feed-forward output projection `wo` is the gated hidden state $\text{GELU}(x W_0) \odot (x W_1)$, which no norm bounds, and it contains the largest values. These values are specific to both tokens and channels. In the first 16 evaluation windows of the base model, 182 of 2,064 token rows entering encoder layer 8's `wo` have a value above 500. All of them are commas, "the" or the end-of-sequence marker, and in each of them the largest value is in the same channel, 888. This is consistent with the massive activations of Sun et al., which appear in a few fixed channels at a few uninformative tokens.

## Methods

### Per-token scales and LLM.int8()

The simplest remedy gives each token its own activation scale, computed at run time from that row's maximum, and each output channel its own weight scale. The integer product of row $i$ and column $j$ is then rescaled by $s_i^X s_j^W$, which kernels can apply after the integer matmul. LLM.int8() calls this vector-wise quantization and combines it with a mixed-precision decomposition. Any input feature dimension in which some value exceeds a threshold (6.0 in the paper) is multiplied in FP16, and the remaining dimensions, more than 99.9% of the values in OPT-scale models, go through INT8. The output is the sum of the two products.

The decomposition reduces speed. The paper's own benchmarks show the INT8 matmuls slower than FP16 up to 6.7B parameters (0.86x at 6.7B) and about 1.8x faster at 175B. End to end, at batch size 1, it measured 253 ms per token against 239 ms for 16-bit. Its contribution was to let large models load in half the memory with no accuracy loss. On T5 it also matches full precision, but it sends far more columns to the FP16 path ([8-bit weights and activations](#8-bit-weights-and-activations)).

### SmoothQuant

Activations are harder to quantize than weights, so [SmoothQuant](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10438) migrates part of the quantization difficulty from the activations to the weights. For a linear layer $Y = XW$, dividing each input channel $j$ by $s_j$ and multiplying the matching row of $W$ by $s_j$ leaves the product unchanged:

$$Y = (X \operatorname{diag}(s)^{-1})\,(\operatorname{diag}(s) W), \qquad s_j = \frac{\max|X_j|^\alpha}{\max|W_j|^{1-\alpha}}$$

$\max|X_j|$ comes from calibration text and $\max|W_j|$ from the weights. At $\alpha = 1$ the activations are flattened completely and the weights absorb the entire range; at $\alpha = 0$ the roles are reversed. The division by $s$ folds into the preceding layer norm's scale or the previous linear layer, so it adds no cost at inference, and both operands then go through ordinary INT8 kernels. The paper uses $\alpha = 0.5$ for OPT and BLOOM, 0.75 for GLM-130B, and its later versions list 0.8 to 0.9 for the Llama family. It reports up to 1.56x faster inference and 2x less memory than FP16. It defines three levels: O1 uses per-token dynamic activation scales, O2 one dynamic scale per tensor, and O3 one static scale per tensor fixed at calibration.

T5 requires α of 0.75 or more for per-tensor INT8 to come within 2% of full-precision perplexity ([8-bit weights and activations](#8-bit-weights-and-activations)). The method also requires a place to fold the scale. For T5's `wo` it would go into the columns of the previous gated layer, which is possible because the gate is elementwise; for inputs that feed several consumers, all of them must share one $s$.

### GPTQ

Weight-only methods keep activations in 16 bits and quantize only the weights. [GPTQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.17323) quantizes one input column of a weight matrix at a time and, after each column, updates the columns not yet quantized to compensate for the error just introduced, measured on calibration inputs. With $H = 2X^\top X$ the Hessian of the layer's squared output error, the optimal compensation for quantizing weight $w_q$ with error $\delta = w_q - \text{quant}(w_q)$ is

$$\Delta w = -\frac{\delta}{[H^{-1}]_{qq}}\, H^{-1}_{:,q}$$

This is the Optimal Brain Surgeon update, carried over from the earlier OBQ method. GPTQ's contribution is to make it efficient at scale. Every row uses the same column order, so all rows share one inverse Hessian. The updates are applied lazily in blocks of 128 columns to raise GPU utilization, and the required rows of $H^{-1}$ come from one Cholesky factorization, with 1% of the mean diagonal added for numerical stability. The paper quantized OPT-175B in about 4 GPU hours on one A100. The [reference code](https://github.com/IST-DASLab/gptq) adds `--act-order`, which quantizes columns in order of decreasing activation size, so that the outlier channels are quantized first, while the largest number of remaining columns can absorb their error.

### AWQ

[AWQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00978) is based on the observation that about 1% of weights affect the output much more than the rest, and that these salient weights are the ones multiplied by large activations rather than the ones with large magnitudes. Keeping those in FP16 would require a mixed-precision kernel, so AWQ instead multiplies input channel $j$ of the weight by $s_j$ before quantizing and divides the activation by $s_j$, folding the division into the previous operation. A salient channel scaled up incurs proportionally less rounding error. The scale is $s = \overline{|X|}^{\,\alpha}$, the mean activation magnitude per channel raised to a power, with $\alpha$ chosen by grid search over $[0, 1]$ to minimize the layer's output error on calibration text. AWQ updates no weights, so calibration is fast and the method is less prone to overfitting the calibration set. It uses groups of 128 weights throughout and received the MLSys 2024 best paper award. [Calibrated low-bit weights](#calibrated-low-bit-weights) compares AWQ and GPTQ on T5.

### FP8

FP8 reduces the effect of outliers by allocating bits to the exponent. An E4M3 value keeps 3 mantissa bits at every magnitude within its range, so under a scale chosen for a large outlier, small values receive coarser relative precision instead of rounding to zero. Hopper added FP8 tensor cores, and vLLM's [online FP8 mode](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/stable/features/quantization/llm_compressor/fp8/#online-dynamic-quantization) runs W8A8 in FP8 without calibration data. It quantizes every linear layer's weights with one scale per tensor and computes the activation scale on each forward pass. [DeepSeek-V3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437) was trained in FP8, with E4M3 for all tensors, activation scales per 1×128 tile and weight scales per 128×128 block, and kept the embedding, output head, norms and attention in higher precision.

### Rotations

Whether a channel is an outlier depends on the coordinate system. Multiplying the activations by an orthogonal matrix $Q$ and the weights by $Q^\top$ leaves the product unchanged, and it turns a vector with one very large coordinate into one whose energy is spread across all coordinates. [QuaRot](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00456) uses randomized Hadamard matrices, most of them folded into the weights and a few applied online, and reaches 4-bit weights, activations and KV cache on Llama-2-70B with WikiText-2 perplexity 3.79 against 3.32 in FP16. [SpinQuant](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16406) learns the rotations instead of sampling them, and reduces the gap to FP16 further.

### The KV cache

At long context the KV cache exceeds the weights in size ([[ml/serving-systems/memory-management#kv-cache-sizing|Memory Management]]), and quantizing it reduces memory per request, so more requests fit in a batch. vLLM supports an [FP8 KV cache](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/stable/features/quantization/quantized_kvcache/) (`kv_cache_dtype="fp8"`) with per-tensor or per-head scales. [KIVI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02750) found that the outliers in keys are concentrated in a few fixed channels while values show no such pattern. It therefore quantizes keys per channel and values per token, down to 2 bits without tuning, and reports about 2.6x less peak memory and 2.35x to 3.47x more throughput on Llama, Falcon and Mistral.

## Measured on T5

The results cited under [Methods](#methods) come mostly from papers on large decoder LLMs. To examine the same effects on a model I could run end to end, I quantized T5 v1.1 base (248M parameters) in numpy and measured how much its perplexity rises when continuing held-out Go blog posts. The encoder receives 128 tokens of a post and the decoder scores the next 64. At full precision the perplexity is 24.37. Each number in the tables is the change relative to that value, on the same 256 windows, and the quoted intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals. The setup, the code and its simplifications are recorded in the collapsed block "How this was measured".

### Weights only, rounded to nearest

Rounding every weight to the nearest grid point, with no calibration, isolates the effect of format and granularity. The Shakespeare column checks that the ranking holds on text very different from the Go blog (its full-precision perplexity is 48.4).

| Weights | Bits per weight | Go blog | Shakespeare |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| INT8, one scale per tensor | 8 | +0.77% | +0.15% |
| INT8, per output channel | 8 | +0.02% | +0.04% |
| INT4, one scale per tensor | 4 | +21,600% | +22,900% |
| INT4, per channel, symmetric | 4 | +5.4% | +5.2% |
| INT4, per channel, asymmetric | 4 | +3.7% | +3.0% |
| INT4, groups of 128 | 4.25 | +2.1% | +1.5% |
| INT4, groups of 32 | 5 | +0.9% | +0.3% |
| INT3, per channel | 3 | +20.1% | +17.0% |
| INT3, groups of 128 | 3.25 | +9.2% | +7.9% |
| INT3, groups of 32 | 4 | +5.2% | +5.3% |
| INT2, groups of 32 | 3 | +58.7% | +51.6% |

With one scale per output channel, INT8 weights cause no measurable loss (+0.02%, interval −0.01% to +0.05%). At 4 bits the loss depends mainly on granularity. One scale per matrix makes the model unusable, a scale per channel costs 4% to 5%, and groups of 128, the group size used in the GPTQ and AWQ papers, reduce the loss to +2.1% (1.7% to 2.5%). Per channel, an asymmetric grid loses less than a symmetric one (3.7% against 5.4%), which is why weight-only kernels store a zero point.

The bits-per-weight column counts the 16-bit scale and zero point each group carries. At equal storage, more bits per weight give a lower loss than smaller groups. INT4 per channel and INT3 in groups of 32 both occupy 4 bits per weight, and the INT4 version loses 3.7% against 5.2%; at 3 bits, INT3 per channel loses 20% and INT2 in groups of 32 loses 59%. This agrees with [Dettmers and Zettlemoyer's](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09720) scaling study, on a much smaller model.

### Calibrated low-bit weights

GPTQ and AWQ use a small amount of calibration text to determine how to round. Each ran on three different calibration sets of 64 windows from the held-out calibration posts; the table gives the mean, with the range across the three in parentheses. Round-to-nearest needs no calibration and has a single result.

| Method, groups of 128 | INT4 | INT3 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| round to nearest | +2.1% | +9.2% |
| AWQ | +1.45% (1.39 to 1.53) | +8.2% (8.1 to 8.3) |
| GPTQ | +1.12% (0.96 to 1.24) | +4.8% (4.6 to 5.0) |

At 4 bits both methods reduce the loss, GPTQ by about half of round-to-nearest's loss and AWQ by about a third, although the difference between them is about the size of one run's confidence interval (roughly ±0.3 points). At 3 bits the difference exceeds that interval. GPTQ halves the loss, and AWQ reduces it by about a tenth. AWQ's search selected α near 0.35 on average, which is a mild scaling. The [AWQ paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00978) reports AWQ slightly ahead of GPTQ on Llama perplexity, so these results do not establish a ranking of the two methods. The model is a 248M encoder-decoder, and both methods are my simplified reimplementations, which take each layer's calibration inputs from the full-precision model. The choice of calibration set had little effect on either method; the three seeds fall within 0.4 points of each other.

### 8-bit weights and activations

Activation quantization is the main source of error for T5 at 8 bits. In every row the weights are INT8 with one scale per output channel, and only the activation handling changes.

| Activation handling | One scale per tensor | One scale per token |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| plain INT8 | +37.7% | +0.56% |
| plain INT8, `wo` inputs left in float | +0.56% | |
| SmoothQuant, α = 0.25 | +66.8% | +0.87% |
| SmoothQuant, α = 0.5 | +7.4% | +0.24% |
| SmoothQuant, α = 0.75 | +1.2% | +0.16% |
| SmoothQuant, α = 0.9 | +1.6% | +0.56% |
| LLM.int8(), threshold 6 | | +0.00% |
| FP8 E4M3 weights and activations | +0.21% | |

One scale per tensor, computed dynamically for each batch, raises perplexity by 38% (95% interval 35% to 40%), and a static scale fixed at calibration gives a similar result (+38.3%). Nearly all of the loss comes from the 24 `wo` inputs among the 216 linear layers. Keeping only those inputs in floating point reduces the loss to +0.56%. Keeping the 108 attention q, k and v inputs in float instead has almost no effect (+36.3%). Per-token scales give the same +0.56% with no layers in floating point, because the largest values are concentrated in a few rows.

SmoothQuant requires a large α on this model. At 0.5, the paper's default for OPT, per-tensor scaling still costs 7.4%. At 0.75 it costs 1.2%, and at 0.9 the loss rises slightly again, to 1.6%, presumably because the weights then carry too much of the range. A small α gives a larger loss than no smoothing (+66.8% at 0.25), since the scale is then set mostly by the weights. Combined with per-token scales, the paper's O1 setting, SmoothQuant reaches +0.16%, the lowest loss among the configurations that keep every activation in INT8. Three calibration seeds gave α = 0.5 per-tensor results between 7.2% and 7.5% and α = 0.75 results between 1.18% and 1.23%, so the result is insensitive to the choice of calibration windows.

At its default threshold of 6, LLM.int8() is indistinguishable from full precision (+0.00%, with an interval of −0.07% to +0.07%). It achieves this by sending 5.7% of all input columns to the floating-point path, averaged over every layer and batch, compared with the 0.1% the paper observed in OPT. At a threshold of 12 it sends 1.3% and costs +0.03%. FP8 requires neither calibration nor special cases. E4M3 for weights and activations, with one dynamic scale per activation tensor, costs +0.21%.

### Precision limits on T5

On T5 v1.1 base, 8-bit weights cause no measurable loss. 8-bit activations cost under 0.6% when the `wo` outliers are handled by per-token scales (with or without SmoothQuant), LLM.int8() or FP8, 1.2% with per-tensor SmoothQuant at α = 0.75, and 38% when they are not handled. 4-bit weights in groups of 128 cost about 1% with GPTQ and 2% without calibration. 3-bit weights cost about 5% with GPTQ. At 2 bits, round-to-nearest raises perplexity by 59% even in groups of 32.

Two limitations apply to carrying these results over to other models. They are costs for a 248M model, while the papers cited under [Methods](#methods) measure models from about 7B to 175B parameters, where the numbers differ. Perplexity on held-out text is also a coarse measure, and a 1% rise gives little information about the change on any particular task.

> [!note]- How this was measured
> - **Code:** all code is in the [`quantization/`](quantization/) directory. [`convert.py`](quantization/convert.py) turns a TensorFlow checkpoint into an `.npz`, [`prep_text.py`](quantization/prep_text.py) cleans the text, [`t5.py`](quantization/t5.py) is the numpy forward pass with a hook on every linear layer, [`quant.py`](quantization/quant.py) holds the formats and methods, [`data.py`](quantization/data.py) builds the windows, and [`run.py`](quantization/run.py) runs the stages (`outliers small|base|large`, `weights`, `calibrated`, `w8a8`, `rows`, `figure`). [`merge.py`](quantization/merge.py) collects them into [`results.json`](quantization/results.json) and prints the tables. [`figure_check.py`](quantization/figure_check.py) recomputes ten of the figure's settings in numpy, and the figure's unit test checks the browser code against them to within $10^{-6}$.
> - **Models:** `t5.1.1.lm100k.{small,base,large}` from `gs://t5-data/pretrained_models/`, the checkpoints linked from the [T5 release page](https://github.com/google-research/text-to-text-transfer-transformer/blob/main/released_checkpoints.md), with 77M, 248M and 783M parameters. The weights are stored in bfloat16 and run in float32. Embeddings, the output head, norms and the two attention products (scores and the weighted sum of values) are never quantized; every other matmul against a weight is.
> - **Text:** the 210 Go blog posts in [golang/website](https://github.com/golang/website) at commit `9d2e951d`, with code blocks, front matter, HTML and link targets stripped (274,290 words), and [tinyshakespeare](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/karpathy/char-rnn/master/data/tinyshakespeare/input.txt). Posts are split by a hash of their file name into 41 posts for calibration and 169 for evaluation, so calibration never sees evaluation text.
> - **Task:** a window is 128 tokens of a post fed to the encoder, then end-of-sequence, and the next 64 tokens scored by the decoder. The evaluation set is 256 such windows from the evaluation posts (16,384 scored tokens), plus 128 Shakespeare windows for the weight-only grid. Full-precision perplexity is 24.37 on the Go blog.
> - **Statistics:** the intervals are 95% paired bootstrap intervals over windows (2,000 resamples) for the change in perplexity against full precision on the same windows. Calibrated methods were run with three calibration seeds, each drawing 64 different windows from the calibration posts.
> - **Simplifications:** quantization is simulated (round to the grid, multiply in float32), so the experiments do not measure speed. AWQ and GPTQ take their calibration inputs from the full-precision model rather than from the already-quantized earlier layers, and each layer is handled independently; AWQ searches 21 values of α and, like the paper, uses asymmetric groups of 128. GPTQ follows the reference code (1% damping, blocks of 128, no `--act-order`). SmoothQuant's weights are INT8 per output channel.
> - **Environment:** Python 3.13, numpy 2.5.3, TensorFlow 2.21 (checkpoint reading only), sentencepiece 0.2.3, on 4 cores of an Intel Xeon at 2.1 GHz. All the stages together take about two and a half hours on that machine.

> [!note]- Potentially interesting ideas
> These ideas arose while running the experiments. None of them was tested, and any of them may be wrong or may already exist in work I have not read.
>
> - **A fixed float column instead of a threshold.** In encoder layer 8, every token row with a giant `wo` input had its largest value in the same channel, 888. If that holds across layers and text, as the [massive activations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17762) paper found for decoder LLMs, keeping a short list of channels per layer in floating point, chosen once at calibration, might retain most of LLM.int8()'s accuracy without a run-time outlier check on every batch. The number of channels the list requires is an open question; in encoder layer 11 the giant rows put their largest value in three different channels.
> - **A threshold relative to the layer.** [LLM.int8()](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07339)'s threshold of 6 was chosen for OPT-style models, where the outlier features sit in inputs that come after a layer norm. T5's `wo` inputs have no norm in front of them. In the base model, the typical `wo` layer has 68% of its input channels exceed 6 at least once over the 64 calibration windows, and in the full runs 5.7% of all input columns went to the float path, so the outlier path no longer handles a small fraction of the columns. A threshold tied to each layer's typical magnitude might transfer better between architectures.
> - **Does the gap keep widening?** The ratio between the top and median `wo` channel grows from 1,359× to 5,452× between the 77M and 783M models. The [FP16 overflow in Flan-T5-XXL](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/issues/20287) is consistent with even larger values at 11B, but I measured only the three smallest sizes.
> - **Why the same channels after every norm?** The inputs right after a norm have small magnitudes, but their largest channel is often the same one across layers (channel 559 in 18 of the base model's 60 normed q and wi inputs, channel 614 in 19 of the large model's 120). Comparing those channels with the norms' learned scale vectors would show whether the norm weights produce them.
