A *fingerprint filter* for a set $S$ of $n$ keys stores an $f$-bit *fingerprint* $\varphi(x)$ of each key $x \in S$ in an array of cells and answers a query for $y$ by comparing $\varphi(y)$ with values read from a few cells chosen by hashing $y$. A key in $S$ always matches. An absent key matches with a probability close to $t \cdot 2^{-f}$, where $t$ is the number of independent comparisons made, so the fingerprint length sets the false-positive rate $\varepsilon$, and the space is $f$ bits, plus any metadata per cell, times the ratio of cells to keys. *Dynamic* filters store each fingerprint in one cell of a hash table and support insertion and deletion: cuckoo filters ([Fan et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2674005.2674994)) and quotient filters ([Bender et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.14778/2350229.2350275)). *Static* filters are built once from the whole of $S$ by solving a linear system over GF(2) in which the equation for $x$ has right-hand side $\varphi(x)$: xor filters ([Graf and Lemire 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3376122)), binary fuse filters ([Graf and Lemire 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3510449)) and ribbon filters ([Dillinger and Walzer 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.02515)). A static filter accepts no insertion after it is built.

Any filter for sets of $n$ keys from a large universe needs at least $n \lg(1/\varepsilon)$ bits, and a [[algorithms/probabilistic/bloom-filters|Bloom filter]] at its best number of hash functions uses $\lg e \approx 1.44$ times that. A fingerprint filter with load $\alpha$, the number of keys per cell, uses about $(\lg(1/\varepsilon) + c)/\alpha$ bits per key for a small constant $c$, so its overhead over the bound is a factor $1/\alpha$ and an additive term. The static filters reach $\alpha$ between 0.81 and 0.94 with $c = 0$. The dynamic filters operate at $\alpha$ between 0.75 and 0.95 with $c$ close to 3.

On the [[algorithms/probabilistic/index|series benchmark]], each filter was built from the 5,843,219 keys of the first hour (the cuckoo and quotient tables also from more keys, enough to fill them to their design loads) and queried with 52,974,808 keys that first appear later in the day. Every filter's false-positive rate is within 1% of its formula, or within two standard errors where a run counts fewer than 1,000 false positives. At $2^{-8} = 0.39\%$ a ribbon filter of width 128 uses 8.56 bits per key, 1.07 times the bound, against 9.02 for a binary fuse filter, 9.84 for an xor filter and 11.5 for a Bloom filter. A cuckoo filter with 4-slot buckets at 95% load uses fewer bits per key than a Bloom filter at rates below about 0.4% (12.63 against 13.09 bits at 0.185%) and more above it. A quotient filter at 90% load reads 28 slots per absent query on average and its queries cost 248 to 337 ns, against 15 to 18 ns for the cuckoo, xor and binary fuse filters.

## The space bound

Let $U$ be a universe much larger than $n$. A structure that answers membership queries for every $n$-key subset of $U$ with no false negatives and false-positive rate $\varepsilon$ must use at least $n \lg(1/\varepsilon)$ bits in the worst case ([Carter et al. 1978](https://doi.org/10.1145/800133.804332)), and static structures exist that use $n \lg(1/\varepsilon)(1 + o(1))$ bits ([Dietzfelbinger and Pagh 2008](https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.3693)). Insertion raises the bound. [Lovett and Porat (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1109/FOCS.2010.81) showed that a dynamic structure, which receives the keys one at a time and must answer queries in between, needs at least $C(\varepsilon) \, n \lg(1/\varepsilon)$ bits for a constant $C(\varepsilon) > 1$ that depends only on $\varepsilon$. If $n$ is not known when the structure is created, [Pagh, Segev and Wieder (2013)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.1188) showed that a further $\Omega(n \log \log n)$ bits are needed.

The rate of a fingerprint filter follows from one assumption: the fingerprint of an absent key is uniform on its $2^f$ values and independent of the values it is compared with. If a query compares $\varphi(y)$ with $t$ stored fingerprints, $\varepsilon = 1 - (1 - 2^{-f})^t \approx t \, 2^{-f}$, so $f \approx \lg(1/\varepsilon) + \lg t$. With $\mu$ metadata bits per cell and load $\alpha$,

$$
\text{bits per key} = \frac{f + \mu}{\alpha} \approx \frac{\lg(1/\varepsilon) + \lg t + \mu}{\alpha} .
$$

| Filter | Comparisons $t$ | Metadata $\mu$ | Load $\alpha$ | Bits per key |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Cuckoo, buckets of $b$ slots | $2b\alpha$ | 0 | 0.95 at $b = 4$ | $(\lg(1/\varepsilon) + \lg(2b\alpha))/\alpha$ |
| Quotient | $\alpha$ | 3 | 0.75 to 0.9 | $(\lg(1/\varepsilon) + \lg \alpha + 3)/\alpha$ |
| Xor | 1 | 0 | $1/1.23$ | $1.23 \lg(1/\varepsilon)$ |
| Binary fuse, 3 positions | 1 | 0 | $1/1.125$ | $1.125 \lg(1/\varepsilon)$ |
| Ribbon, overhead $\delta$ | 1 | 0 | $1/(1+\delta)$ | $(1 + \delta)\lg(1/\varepsilon)$ |

For the static filters a query computes one value from several cells and compares it with $\varphi(y)$ once, so $t = 1$ and $\varepsilon = 2^{-f}$ exactly when the value is uniform. A quotient filter stores the low bits of a $(q + r)$-bit hash, and its rate is that of a comparison with all $n$ stored hashes, which gives the $t = \alpha$ in the table [below](#quotient-filters). The static filters have a multiplicative overhead and no additive one, and the dynamic filters have both.

## Cuckoo filters

A cuckoo filter is a table of $m$ buckets, each holding $b$ slots of $f$ bits, with 0 reserved for an empty slot. Key $x$ has a nonzero fingerprint $\varphi(x)$ and two candidate buckets,

$$
i_1(x) = h(x) \bmod m, \qquad i_2(x) = i_1(x) \oplus \big(h'(\varphi(x)) \bmod m\big),
$$

for $m$ a power of two and hash functions $h$, $h'$. Since $i_1 = i_2 \oplus (h'(\varphi(x)) \bmod m)$, either bucket together with the fingerprint determines the other, so a fingerprint can be moved between its two buckets without the key, which the filter does not store. Fan et al. call this *partial-key cuckoo hashing*. Insertion stores $\varphi(x)$ in a free slot of $i_1$ or $i_2$ if there is one. Otherwise it removes a fingerprint from a randomly chosen slot of one of the two buckets, stores $\varphi(x)$ there, and inserts the removed fingerprint into its alternate bucket in the same way, for at most 500 relocations (`MaxNumKicks` in the paper); if the walk ends without a free slot, the insertion fails and the table is full. A query reports $x$ as possibly present if either bucket holds $\varphi(x)$, and deletion removes one copy of $\varphi(x)$ from either bucket. The relocation walk is that of cuckoo hashing ([Pagh and Rodler 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jalgor.2003.12.002)), with $b$ slots per bucket.

A query compares $\varphi(y)$ with the $2b$ slots of two buckets, of which about $2b\alpha$ are occupied at load $\alpha = n/(bm)$. With fingerprints uniform on the $2^f - 1$ nonzero values the rate is $1 - (1 - 1/(2^f - 1))^{2b\alpha}$, and the space is $f/\alpha$ bits per key. For $b = 4$ and $\alpha = 0.955$ Fan et al. give $(\lg(1/\varepsilon) + 3)/\alpha$ bits per key. Their *semi-sorting* variant sorts the four fingerprints of a bucket and stores the multiset of their four high-order 4-bit groups as a 12-bit index into a table of the 3,876 possible multisets, saving 1 bit per slot; the space becomes $(\lg(1/\varepsilon) + 2)/\alpha$. At $\alpha = 0.955$ the plain table uses fewer bits per key than a Bloom filter at its best $k$ at rates of 0.37% and below (11.52 against 11.64 bits at $f = 11$, where $\varepsilon = 0.37\%$), and the semi-sorted table below about 3% (7.33 bits for both at $f = 8$), the threshold that Fan et al. report.

Deleting is safe only for a key that was inserted, since an absent key that matches a stored fingerprint would delete another key's fingerprint and create a false negative. All copies of one key go to the same two buckets, so a key inserted more than $2b$ times exhausts them and the insertion fails.

### How full the table gets

A cuckoo table is full when the walk fails, and the load at which that happens depends on $b$, on the table size and on $f$. Two limits bound it. For two choices and buckets of $b$ slots, a placement of all keys exists with probability tending to 1 as $m \to \infty$ if the load is below a threshold $c^*_{2,b}$ and with probability tending to 0 above it; $c^*_{2,1} = 0.5$, $c^*_{2,2} = 0.8970$ and $c^*_{2,4} = 0.9804$ ([Walzer 2021](https://doi.org/10.1137/1.9781611976465.131), Table 1). A walk of at most 500 relocations finds a placement only when one is reachable within that many steps, so the load at the first failure lies below the threshold. Each line below is the mean load at the first failed insertion over 10 builds with 16-bit fingerprints, inserting the benchmark keys in order of first appearance.

| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4,096 | 0.532 | 0.879 | 0.970 | 0.994 |
| 65,536 | 0.502 | 0.876 | 0.965 | 0.992 |
| 1,048,576 | 0.502 | 0.867 | 0.962 | 0.988 |
| 8,388,608 | 0.499 | 0.864 | 0.959 | 0.987 |

The load at the first failure falls as the table grows, by 1.5 points from 4,096 to 8.4 million slots for $b = 2$ and by 1.1 points for $b = 4$. Fan et al. report 50%, 84%, 95% and 98% for $b = 1, 2, 4, 8$. With $b = 4$ on $2^{23}$ slots the 10 fills averaged 1.09 to 1.30 relocations per insertion up to the first failure.

Partial-key cuckoo hashing adds a dependence on $f$: the alternate bucket of a fingerprint in bucket $i$ is one of at most $2^f - 1$ buckets, so the keys of a bucket can move only within a neighborhood whose size does not grow with $m$. Fan et al. show that a high load requires $f = \Omega(\log n / b)$. With $b = 4$ and $2^{23}$ slots, 4-bit fingerprints fail at a mean load of 0.847 (lowest 0.693, highest 0.945 of 10 builds), 5-bit fingerprints at 0.953 and 6- to 16-bit fingerprints at 0.955 to 0.959; on $2^{20}$ slots 4-bit fingerprints reach 0.949. Replacing the xor by $i_2 = (h'(\varphi(x)) - i_1) \bmod m$, an involution that allows any $m$, gave 0.957 at 16 bits and 0.885 at 4 bits on $2^{23}$ slots.

## Quotient filters

A quotient filter stores a $p$-bit hash $\hat h(x)$ of each key, with $p = q + r$, by *quotienting* ([Cleary 1984](https://doi.org/10.1109/TC.1984.1676499)): the high $q$ bits, the *quotient* $h_q(x)$, select a home slot in a table of $2^q$ slots, and only the low $r$ bits, the *remainder* $h_r(x)$, are stored. The remainders of all keys with the same quotient are stored in consecutive slots, a *run*, sorted by remainder. Runs appear in the order of their quotients, and a run whose home slot is taken is shifted to the right, as in linear probing. A maximal sequence of occupied slots is a *cluster*. Bender et al. keep three bits per slot:

- **`is_occupied`:** some stored key has this slot as its home slot.
- **`is_continuation`:** the slot holds a remainder of the same run as the slot before it.
- **`is_shifted`:** the remainder in the slot is not in its home slot.

A query for $y$ checks `is_occupied` at $h_q(y)$ and, if it is set, scans left to the start of the cluster, counts the occupied home slots it passed to determine which run belongs to $h_q(y)$, scans right to the start of that run, and compares $h_r(y)$ with each remainder in the run. Insertion and deletion locate the run the same way and shift the rest of the cluster by one slot.

The filter represents the multiset of $p$-bit hashes exactly, so it errs only when an absent key's hash equals a stored one: $\varepsilon = 1 - (1 - 2^{-p})^n \approx 1 - e^{-\alpha / 2^r} \le \alpha \, 2^{-r}$ at load $\alpha = n/2^q$. A slot holds $r + 3$ bits, so the space is $(r + 3)/\alpha$ bits per key. Because the stored remainders, read in slot order, are the stored hashes in sorted order, two filters with the same $q + r$ can be merged by a single sequential pass that writes a new table, and the table can be doubled by moving one bit from each remainder to the quotient. Bender et al. use merging to maintain filters on flash storage with sequential writes.

The cost of a query is the length of the scan, which grows with the length of clusters, as in linear probing. Bender et al. bound the expected cluster length by $1/(1 - \alpha e^{1 - \alpha})$, which is 27 at $\alpha = 3/4$, and recommend filling the table to at most 75%. ## Xor filters

An xor filter for $S$ is an array $B$ of $c$ cells of $f$ bits, divided into three blocks of $c/3$ cells, with hash functions $h_0, h_1, h_2$ that map each key to one cell in each block, such that

$$
B[h_0(x)] \oplus B[h_1(x)] \oplus B[h_2(x)] = \varphi(x) \qquad \text{for every } x \in S .
$$

A query computes the left side for $y$ and compares it with $\varphi(y)$. For $y \notin S$, the left side is a fixed value and $\varphi(y)$ is uniform and independent of it, so $\varepsilon = 2^{-f}$. Graf and Lemire use $c = \lfloor 1.23 n \rfloor + 32$, so the space is $1.23 f$ bits per key plus $32f/n$.

The equations are a linear system over GF(2) with one row per key and three nonzero entries per row. Graf and Lemire solve it by *peeling*, the method [Botelho, Pagh and Ziviani (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-73951-7_13) used for minimal perfect hashing. View the keys as hyperedges on the $c$ cells. Repeatedly find a cell that lies in exactly one remaining key, push that key and that cell onto a stack, and remove the key. If every key is removed, pop the stack and set each key's cell to the value that satisfies its equation: no key popped later touches the cell, because each of them was removed earlier, when the cell already had degree 1. If peeling stops with keys remaining, those keys form the 2-core of the hypergraph, the largest subhypergraph in which every cell has degree at least 2, and the build is repeated with new hash functions.

For random 3-uniform hypergraphs the 2-core is empty with probability tending to 1 if $n/c < c_3 \approx 0.81847$ and nonempty with probability tending to 1 if $n/c > c_3$ ([Molloy 2005](https://doi.org/10.1002/rsa.20061)), so $c/n$ must exceed $1/c_3 \approx 1.2218$, and the factor 1.23 leaves 0.7% of margin. ## Binary fuse filters

A binary fuse filter replaces the three blocks by a sequence of $s$ *segments* of $L$ cells. Key $x$ chooses a starting segment $j(x)$ uniformly from the first $s - 2$, and its three cells lie one in each of segments $j$, $j + 1$ and $j + 2$. The array is filled by peeling and assignment exactly as for an xor filter, and $\varepsilon = 2^{-f}$. Hypergraphs with this banded structure are a case of *spatial coupling*: the cells near the two ends of the array have fewer keys, peeling starts there, and it proceeds inward segment by segment. For 3 cells per key, [Dietzfelbinger and Walzer (2019)](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.ESA.2019.38) and Walzer (2021) show that as $s$ and $L$ grow such hypergraphs peel up to a load of $c^*_3 \approx 0.917935$, the threshold for the existence of a solution of the system, against 0.818 for the unstructured hypergraph of an xor filter.

Graf and Lemire set $L = 2^{\lfloor \log_{3.33} n + 2.25 \rfloor}$, capped at $2^{18}$, and the array length to $\max(1.125, \; 0.875 + 0.25 \ln(10^6)/\ln n)$ times $n$ rounded up to a whole number of segments, which is 1.125 for $n \ge 10^6$. They report 9.0 bits per key with 8-bit fingerprints, and 8.6 for a 4-cell variant with factor 1.075. For the 5,843,219 keys of the hour, $L = 32{,}768$ and the array has 201 segments, 6,586,368 cells or a factor of 1.127. The rates are 3,908.1 per million at $f = 8$ and 15.3 at $f = 16$, at 9.02 and 18.03 bits per key.

At 5.8 million keys every build succeeded from factor 1.12, 3 points above the asymptotic $1/c^*_3 = 1.0894$, and at a million keys from 1.13. Below about $10^5$ keys the array length changes only in steps of one segment, so several factors give the same array, and at $10^4$ keys about 75% of builds succeed from 1.18 to 1.22 and 98% from 1.23. Fewer and shorter segments move the peeling threshold away from $c^*_3$, which the size formula accounts for with its larger factor at small $n$.

## Ribbon filters

A ribbon filter solves a linear system whose rows are *bands*. Each key $x$ has a start $s(x)$ uniform on $\{0, \dots, m - w\}$ and a $w$-bit coefficient vector $c(x)$ whose first bit is 1, and its row of the $m$-column system is $c(x)$ placed at columns $s(x)$ to $s(x) + w - 1$. The filter stores an $m \times r$ bit matrix $Z$ such that

$$
\textstyle\bigoplus_{j=0}^{w-1} c_j(x) \, Z[s(x) + j] = \varphi(x) \qquad \text{for every } x \in S,
$$

where $Z[i]$ is row $i$ of $Z$ and $\varphi(x)$ has $r$ bits ([Dillinger and Walzer 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.02515)). A query computes the left side for $y$. With $Z$ stored column by column in $w$-bit words, each of the $r$ bits of the left side is the parity of one AND of $c(y)$ with $w$ contiguous bits of one column, which reads two or three 64-bit words per column for $w \le 128$, all within a range of $w$ consecutive rows. The rate is $2^{-r}$. With $m = (1 + \delta) n$ the space is $(1 + \delta) r$ bits per key; Dillinger and Walzer write $\varepsilon$ for the overhead, called $\delta$ here to keep $\varepsilon$ for the false-positive rate.

Construction is Gaussian elimination in the order of the keys, which the paper calls on-the-fly banding. The solver keeps at most one stored row per column $i$, with its leading 1 in column $i$. A new row is placed at the column of its leading 1 if that column has no stored row. Otherwise the stored row is xored into it, together with the right-hand sides, and the result, whose leading 1 has moved right, is placed in the same way. A row that becomes 0 is linearly dependent on earlier rows, and since its right-hand side is a random fingerprint, the system then has no solution with probability $1 - 2^{-r}$; the build is repeated with new hash functions. After all rows are placed, back substitution from the last column determines $Z$. Each step touches one band of $w$ columns, and sorting the keys by start first keeps the stored rows that a key meets in a small range of memory.

Dillinger and Walzer prove that construction succeeds with high probability if $w$ is at least about $(\log n)/\delta$, so for a fixed width the overhead needed grows with $n$. The measured overhead at which half of the builds succeed rises with $n$ for every width:

| Keys | $w = 32$ | $w = 64$ | $w = 128$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 10,000 | 0.15 (83%) | 0.04 (70%) | 0.015 (80%) |
| 100,000 | 0.20 (75%) | 0.07 (74%) | 0.02 (50%) |
| 1,000,000 | 0.30 (85%) | 0.10 (70%) | 0.04 (70%) |
| 5,843,219 | 0.35 (80%) | 0.12 (80%) | 0.05 (60%) |

Each cell gives the smallest overhead $\delta$ tried at which at least half of the builds succeeded, with the fraction that did; trials were 200, 100, 40 and 10 builds for the four sizes. At $w = 32$ a residual failure rate remains at large overhead: 1 of 40 builds of a million keys and 1 of 10 of 5.8 million keys failed at $\delta = 0.5$. The paper's own measurements put the overhead of this *standard* ribbon at 14 to 20% for $w = 64$ and 6 to 8% for $w = 128$ at a 1% rate.

At full scale I used $\delta = 0.07$ with $w = 128$ and $\delta = 0.15$ with $w = 64$, and all 15 builds of each succeeded on the first set of hash functions.

| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 128, 0.07 | 7 | 7.49 | 7,816.3 | 7,812.5 |
| 128, 0.07 | 8 | 8.56 | 3,914.8 | 3,906.3 |
| 128, 0.07 | 10 | 10.70 | 976.1 | 976.6 |
| 128, 0.07 | 13 | 13.91 | 123.2 | 122.1 |

The ratio of bits per key to $\lg(1/\varepsilon)$ is 1.070 for every $r$ at $w = 128$ and 1.15 at $w = 64$. A Bloom filter with the rate of the 7-bit ribbon, 0.78%, needs 10.1 bits per key.

Dillinger and Walzer also describe *homogeneous* ribbon, whose system is solvable for every key set, so construction never fails, at the cost of a rate slightly above $2^{-r}$ that depends on the overhead. In *bumped ribbon retrieval* ([Dillinger et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.SEA.2022.4)) keys whose band would overflow are removed to a second, smaller structure, and the overhead falls below 1%. RocksDB added a ribbon filter as an experimental option in version 6.15.0, released in November 2020, with a space saving of about 30% against its Bloom filter at the same rate and 3 to 4 times the construction cost ([`HISTORY.md`](https://github.com/facebook/rocksdb/blob/main/HISTORY.md)).

## Cost of building and querying

The timings are single-threaded on one pinned core, in nanoseconds per key, medians of 3 repetitions; the range over the repetitions follows in parentheses where it exceeds 10% of the median. Each filter is built from the hour's keys (the dynamic tables from as many keys as fill them to the stated load) and queried with 5,843,219 present keys in shuffled order and 5,843,219 absent keys. The two Bloom filters have 12 bits per key and $k = 8$, with the [[algorithms/probabilistic/bloom-filters#Blocked filters|blocked]] layout's bits in one 512-bit line.

| Filter | Size | Build | Present query | Absent query |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Bloom, standard | 8.4 MiB | 56 (50 to 64) | 59 | 45 (39 to 45) |
| Bloom, blocked 512 | 8.4 MiB | 50 | 50 (49 to 57) | 39 |
| Cuckoo, $b = 4$, $f = 12$, $\alpha = 0.95$ | 16 MiB | 103 (91 to 104) | 15 (13 to 16) | 16 (14 to 18) |
| Quotient, $r = 8$, $\alpha = 0.75$ | 16 MiB | 39 | 82 (67 to 85) | 49 |
| Quotient, $r = 8$, $\alpha = 0.9$ | 16 MiB | 59 | 337 (324 to 388) | 248 (221 to 251) |
| Xor, $f = 8$ | 6.9 MiB | 218 (199 to 219) | 18 | 16 |
| Binary fuse, $f = 8$ | 6.3 MiB | 172 (154 to 175) | 18 | 17 |
| Ribbon, $w = 128$, $r = 8$ | 6.0 MiB | 341 (327 to 364) | 32 (29 to 35) | 33 (28 to 35) |
| Ribbon, $w = 64$, $r = 8$ | 6.4 MiB | 130 (120 to 135) | 42 | 41 (36 to 46) |

Every filter exceeds the 2 MiB per-core L2 cache and fits in the 260 MiB last-level cache. Queries are independent of one another, so the processor overlaps their cache misses and the figures are throughput. The cuckoo and quotient filters of the harness store each slot in 16 bits instead of packing 12 or 11, and their sizes are those of the arrays as stored.

A cuckoo query reads two buckets of 8 bytes and an xor or binary fuse query reads three bytes at independent positions, and these cost 15 to 18 ns. A quotient filter query scans its cluster, and its cost tracks the cluster lengths [measured above](#quotient-filters): at 90% load an absent query reads 27.7 slots on average and costs 248 ns, and a present query, which lands in a cluster of 109 slots on average, costs 337 ns; at 75% load the two cost 49 and 82 ns. The Bloom filters of this harness evaluate a 64-bit mix per probe and branch at the first 0 bit, and their queries cost 39 to 59 ns. [Lang et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.14778/3303753.3303757) find with vectorized implementations that blocked Bloom filters outperform cuckoo filters at high lookup throughput; the timings here use scalar code for every filter and do not test that comparison.

Construction is where the static filters cost more. Peeling an xor filter costs 218 ns per key, and a binary fuse filter 172 ns; in both the peeling passes visit the cells of each key at independent positions, and in the binary fuse filter those positions lie within three adjacent segments. The ribbon filter of width 128 costs 341 ns per key, 3.3 times an insertion into the cuckoo table at 95% load (103 ns) and 6.1 times one into the standard Bloom filter (56 ns), and that of width 64 costs 130 ns. The quotient filter's build costs 39 and 59 ns per key at the two loads because the cost is averaged over the whole fill, and most insertions are made at loads where they shift few slots; the shift counts in the [cluster table](#quotient-filters) give the cost of an insertion at each load.

## Later designs

Several designs since 2020 address the dynamic case. The *prefix filter* ([Even, Even and Morrison 2022](https://doi.org/10.14778/3523210.3523211)) stores most keys in a table of small per-bin dictionaries, one cache line per bin, and the keys that overflow a bin in a second, smaller filter, so that most queries read one cache line. The *vector quotient filter* ([Pandey et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3448016.3452841)) places each key in one of two blocks of small quotient filters, chosen by load, and searches a block with vector instructions. *InfiniFilter* ([Dayan et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3589285)) and the *Aleph filter* ([Dayan, Bercea and Pagh 2024](https://doi.org/10.14778/3681954.3682027)) expand a quotient-style filter as keys arrive, without knowing $n$ in advance, which is the case that the Pagh, Segev and Wieder bound concerns; InfiniFilter assigns longer fingerprints to newer keys so that the rate stays bounded, and the Aleph filter makes its operations take constant time.

## Consequences for implementations

For a set known in full at build time, such as the keys of an immutable table file, a static filter has a smaller size at every rate measured. A ribbon filter of width 128 at 7% overhead has the smallest size, 1.07 times the bound against 1.127 for a binary fuse filter and 1.23 for an xor filter, and costs 2.0 times as much to build per key as a binary fuse filter and 1.8 to 1.9 times as much per query. At 5.8 million keys a width-64 ribbon filter needed 12 to 15% overhead to build reliably, which places it at or above the binary fuse filter in space. Against a Bloom filter at the same rate, a binary fuse filter saves 22% of the space at both $2^{-8}$ and $2^{-16}$, with queries 2.3 to 3.3 times faster in this harness and construction about 3 times slower.

When keys must be inserted after the filter is created, a cuckoo filter with 4-slot buckets at 95% load uses less space than a Bloom filter only at rates below about 0.4%, or about 3% with semi-sorting, and the table must be sized for the largest $n$, since an insertion fails once the walk does not find a free slot; at 8.4 million slots that happened at a load of 0.953 to 0.961 for fingerprints of 6 to 16 bits. Its fingerprints must have at least 5 bits for a table of that size, and inserting the same key more than 8 times fails. A quotient filter supports merging and doubling, which a cuckoo filter does not, but its query cost grows with load: 4.2 slots per absent query at 75% load and 28 at 90%, where the space advantage over a Bloom filter begins only at rates below about 0.1%. The counting quotient filter's 2.125 metadata bits and rank-and-select lookup are designed to remove that trade-off.

> - **Filters.** [`filters.h`](filter-experiments/filters.h) implements each filter; every hash is `fmix64(x ^ seed)` with a seed from a SplitMix64 stream, reduced to a range by a 128-bit multiply-high. The cuckoo filter takes $i_1$ from the high bits of one hash and the fingerprint from bits 40 and up (low bits for the non-power-of-two variant), with 0 mapped to 1, and $h'$ is `fmix64` of the fingerprint times a constant. The quotient filter stores remainders unsorted within a run, which changes no rate and no cluster length. The xor and binary fuse filters take their three positions from one hash by multiply-high on rotations of it. The ribbon filters sort keys by start with a counting sort and store the solution in interleaved 64-bit column blocks. [`selftest.c`](filter-experiments/selftest.c) checks every filter for false negatives and its rate against the formula on 200,000 random keys.
> - **Experiments.** [`exp.c`](filter-experiments/exp.c) runs mode F (space and rate, 3 seeds, [`F.txt`](filter-experiments/F.txt)), G (ribbon filters at full scale, [`G.txt`](filter-experiments/G.txt)), L (cuckoo load at the first failure, 10 seeds, [`L.txt`](filter-experiments/L.txt)), Q (quotient filter probes, shifts and clusters on $2^{22}$ slots, 5 seeds, [`Q.txt`](filter-experiments/Q.txt)), X (xor and binary fuse construction against size factor, [`X.txt`](filter-experiments/X.txt)), R (ribbon construction against overhead and width, [`R.txt`](filter-experiments/R.txt)) and J (the figure's six layouts at 4,096 slots, 400 builds per load, [`J.txt`](filter-experiments/J.txt)). > [!note]- Potentially interesting ideas
> - **Semi-sorted cuckoo buckets.** The crossover with the Bloom filter at about 3% that semi-sorting gives was computed from the formula above and not measured; the encoding also changes the cost of a query, which decodes a 12-bit index per bucket.
> - **Counting quotient filter at 95% load.** Measuring the rank-and-select layout of Pandey et al. at the loads in the cluster table would show how much of the 248 ns query at 90% load it removes.
> - **The 4-cell binary fuse filter.** Graf and Lemire's 4-cell variant at factor 1.075 would place a peeled filter within 0.08 of the ribbon filter's 1.07 ratio, with a fourth memory access per query.
> - **Bumped ribbon at full scale.** The overhead of standard ribbon grows with $n$ in the runs above; bumped ribbon removes that growth and would show the space attainable at 5.8 million keys with a width of 64.
> - **Isolated lookups.** As in the Bloom filter timings, the queries here are independent. A chain of dependent lookups would measure latency, where the three independent reads of an xor filter and the scan of a quotient filter behave differently from their throughput.
