An eigenvector of a square matrix $A$ is a nonzero vector $v$ that $A$ maps to a scalar multiple of itself, $Av = \lambda v$, and the scalar $\lambda$ is the corresponding eigenvalue. A matrix is diagonalizable when its eigenvectors form a basis of the space, and in that basis it acts as a diagonal matrix.

Multiplication by a matrix changes the direction of most vectors, and an eigenvector is a vector whose direction is preserved. When the eigenvectors span the space, they determine the behavior of the matrix under repeated application, including the powers $A^k$ and the recurrence $x_{k+1} = Ax_k$. Power iteration, the simplest algorithm based on this behavior, converges at a rate set by the ratio of the two largest eigenvalue magnitudes. Measured step counts on controlled matrices, on the principal components of real datasets and on PageRank for this site's link graph follow the predicted dependence on that ratio. Broader matrix background is in [[math/linear-algebra/cheatsheet|Matrix Theory]], and the [[math/linear-algebra/svd-and-pseudoinverse|SVD note]] continues from the symmetric case.

## Eigenpairs are invariant directions

A square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ changes the direction of most vectors. An eigenvector is a direction that the map preserves, a nonzero $v$ with

$$
Av = \lambda v
$$

for some scalar $\lambda$, the eigenvalue ([ILA §5.1](https://textbooks.math.gatech.edu/ila/eigenvectors.html)). On the line spanned by $v$, the action of $A$ reduces to multiplication by $\lambda$. If such directions form a basis, $A$ is determined entirely by its eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Rearranging $Av = \lambda v$ gives $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$, so $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue exactly when $A - \lambda I$ is singular, which happens exactly when

$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = 0.
$$

The left side is the characteristic polynomial, of degree $n$ in $\lambda$, so $A$ has $n$ eigenvalues counted with multiplicity, possibly complex ([ILA §5.2](https://textbooks.math.gatech.edu/ila/characteristic-polynomial.html)). Each eigenvalue has two multiplicities. The algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$ is its multiplicity as a root. The geometric multiplicity is $\dim \ker(A - \lambda I)$, the number of independent eigenvectors it contributes. The geometric multiplicity never exceeds the algebraic multiplicity.

Eigenvalues of small matrices are computed by hand from the characteristic polynomial, but numerical software does not compute them this way. Finding the roots of a polynomial from its coefficients is badly conditioned even when the eigenvalues themselves are not, and the [Abel-Ruffini theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel%E2%80%93Ruffini_theorem) rules out a formula for degree 5 and up, so every eigenvalue algorithm for general matrices is iterative ([Trefethen and Bau, lecture 25](https://doi.org/10.1137/1.9780898719574)). Power iteration is the simplest of them.

## Diagonalization is a change of basis

Suppose $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$. Stack the eigenvectors as columns of $P$ and the eigenvalues into $D = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$. Then $AP = PD$ column by column, and since $P$ is invertible,

$$
A = P D P^{-1}.
$$

Read from right to left, $P^{-1}$ rewrites a vector in eigenvector coordinates, $D$ scales each coordinate independently, and $P$ maps the result back to the original coordinates. In the eigenvector basis, $A$ acts as $n$ independent scalar multiplications.

> [!tip] Diagonalization is a change of basis
> $A = PDP^{-1}$ says $A$ and $D$ are the same linear map written in different coordinates. Any quantity that similarity preserves, such as the eigenvalues, determinant, trace and rank, can be read directly from $D$. A computation that applies $A$ repeatedly (powers, exponentials, recurrences) can be carried out as scalar arithmetic in the eigenbasis and transformed back.

$A$ is diagonalizable if and only if it has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors, which is the same as every eigenvalue's geometric multiplicity matching its algebraic multiplicity ([ILA §5.4](https://textbooks.math.gatech.edu/ila/diagonalization.html)). Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are always independent, so a matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues is automatically diagonalizable. Diagonalizability fails when a repeated eigenvalue has fewer independent eigenvectors than its algebraic multiplicity. For example, $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\lambda = 1$ with algebraic multiplicity 2 but only one eigenvector direction, so it is defective.

> [!warning] Not every square matrix is diagonalizable
> Having $n$ eigenvalues with multiplicity does not mean having $n$ independent eigenvectors. A defective matrix like $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has no eigenbasis, so $A = PDP^{-1}$ does not exist for it, and an argument that begins by diagonalizing $A$ does not apply to it. The Jordan form takes the place of diagonalization in theory, and the Schur decomposition in numerical practice. Nearly defective matrices cause a related problem in floating point. $P$ exists but is close to singular, so $P^{-1}$ amplifies error, as discussed under [NumPy caveats](#numpy-and-numerical-caveats).

If $A = A^T$, every eigenvalue is real, eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal, and an orthonormal eigenbasis exists. This is the spectral theorem, $A = Q D Q^T$ with $Q$ orthogonal ([Strang, 18.06 lecture 25](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06-linear-algebra-spring-2010/resources/lecture-25-symmetric-matrices-and-positive-definiteness/)). Covariance matrices, graph Laplacians and Hessians are symmetric, so a large part of applied linear algebra concerns this case.

## Worked 2x2 example

Take the symmetric matrix

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The characteristic polynomial is $\det(A - \lambda I) = (3-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 8 = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 4)$, so the eigenvalues are 4 and 2.

For $\lambda = 4$, the matrix $A - 4I = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ has kernel spanned by $(1, 1)^T$.

For $\lambda = 2$, the matrix $A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has kernel spanned by $(1, -1)^T$.

The eigenvectors are orthogonal, as the spectral theorem guarantees. Normalizing gives $Q = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $A = Q\,\mathrm{diag}(4, 2)\,Q^T$. Geometrically, $A$ stretches by 4 along the diagonal $(1,1)$ and by 2 along the anti-diagonal $(1,-1)$. After ten applications the component along the diagonal has grown by a factor of $4^{10} / 2^{10} = 1024$ relative to the other component, so the iterate from almost any starting vector points nearly along $(1,1)$. Power iteration is based on this observation.

## Matrix powers and stability

Diagonalization reduces repeated application of $A$ to repeated scalar multiplication:

$$
A^k = (P D P^{-1})^k = P D^k P^{-1}, \qquad D^k = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1^k, \dots, \lambda_n^k),
$$

because the inner $P^{-1}P$ pairs cancel. With a starting vector written in eigencoordinates as $x_0 = \sum_i c_i v_i$, the iterates $x_k = A^k x_0$ are

$$
x_k = \sum_i c_i \lambda_i^k v_i.
$$

Each mode evolves independently. A mode with $|\lambda_i| < 1$ decays and a mode with $|\lambda_i| > 1$ grows, provided $x_0$ has some of it ($c_i \neq 0$). The recurrence $x_{k+1} = Ax_k$ for a diagonalizable $A$ therefore converges to zero from every start exactly when the spectral radius $\rho(A) = \max_i |\lambda_i|$ is below 1. This is Strang's treatment of difference equations in [18.06 lecture 22](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06-linear-algebra-spring-2010/resources/lecture-22-diagonalization-and-powers-of-a/), and [lecture 24](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06-linear-algebra-spring-2010/resources/lecture-24-markov-matrices-fourier-series/) applies it to Markov matrices, whose largest eigenvalue is exactly 1. The same argument gives the convergence rate of [[math/numerical-optimization#gradient-descent-and-conditioning|gradient descent]]. On a quadratic, each step multiplies the error by $I - \alpha H$, and the rate $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$ is the spectral radius of that matrix at the optimal step size.

The eigenvalue analysis has two limitations. A defective matrix has Jordan blocks, and a block contributes terms like $k \lambda^{k-1}$, so $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}^k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ grows linearly although both eigenvalues are 1. Second, for a non-symmetric matrix with $\rho(A) < 1$, $\lVert A^k \rVert$ can grow by orders of magnitude before it decays, because with a badly conditioned $P$ the modes can cancel initially and cease to cancel later. The eigenvalues determine the limit of $A^k$ and give little information about its transient behavior (Trefethen and Embree, *Spectra and Pseudospectra*, 2005).

## Power iteration and the eigenvalue gap

Suppose the largest eigenvalue is strictly larger in magnitude than the rest, $|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2| \ge \dots \ge |\lambda_n|$. Dividing the expansion of $x_k$ by $\lambda_1^k$ gives

$$
\frac{x_k}{\lambda_1^k} = c_1 v_1 + \sum_{i \ge 2} c_i \left(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\right)^k v_i .
$$

Every term except the first decays, and the slowest decays by a factor of $r = |\lambda_2 / \lambda_1|$ per step. Repeated multiplication by $A$ with rescaling therefore converges to the direction of the top eigenvector, provided the start has a nonzero component along it ($c_1 \ne 0$, which holds with probability 1 for a random start). Richard von Mises described the method in 1929 ([von Mises and Pollaczek-Geiringer](https://doi.org/10.1002/zamm.19290090105)). It is still the inner loop of PageRank, of spectral normalization in GANs, which runs one step per training step and reuses the vector ([Miyato et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05957)), and of the power steps in [[math/linear-algebra/svd-and-pseudoinverse#randomized-svd|randomized SVD]].

```python
import numpy as np

def power_iteration(A, steps, seed=0):
    x = np.random.default_rng(seed).standard_normal(A.shape[0])
    for _ in range(steps):
        x = A @ x
        x /= np.linalg.norm(x)      # rescale so nothing overflows
    return x, x @ A @ x             # eigenvector estimate, Rayleigh quotient
```

For a symmetric matrix, with $\theta_k$ the angle between $x_k$ and $v_1$, the expansion gives

$$
\tan \theta_k \le \tan \theta_0 \, r^k, \qquad r = \left|\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right|.
$$

Trefethen and Bau state the rate as Theorem 27.1 ([lecture 27](https://doi.org/10.1137/1.9780898719574)), and Golub and Van Loan give a bound of this form in §8.2, with the non-symmetric power method in §7.3. On a log scale the error falls along a straight line whose slope is $\log r$. The figure runs the iteration on a $40 \times 40$ matrix with an adjustable $r$. ### Step count as the gap closes

Solving the bound for a tolerance $\varepsilon$ gives the step count

$$
k \approx \frac{\ln(\tan\theta_0 / \varepsilon)}{\ln(1/r)} \approx \frac{\ln(\tan\theta_0 / \varepsilon)}{1 - r} \quad \text{as } r \to 1.
$$

The work grows like $1/(1-r)$, so halving the gap doubles the number of steps. I measured it on symmetric $200 \times 200$ matrices with $\lambda_1 = 1$, $\lambda_2 = r$ and the other 198 eigenvalues uniform in $[-r, r]$, ten random matrices and starts per row, counting steps until $\sin \theta_k \le 10^{-8}$:

| $r$ | Steps, median (range) | Bound | Steps × $(1 - r)$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0.1 | 9.5 (9 to 10) | 10 | 8.6 |
| 0.5 | 30 (27 to 32) | 33 | 15.0 |
| 0.8 | 87.5 (80 to 97) | 101.5 | 17.5 |
| 0.9 | 181 (168 to 200) | 215 | 18.1 |
| 0.95 | 367.5 (315 to 392) | 440.5 | 18.4 |
| 0.99 | 1825 (1562 to 1943) | 2246 | 18.3 |
| 0.995 | 3659 (3132 to 3887) | 4502.5 | 18.3 |

The median measured contraction per step, taken after the faster modes have decayed, came within 0.012 of $r$ in every row (0.488 at $r = 0.5$, 0.990 at $r = 0.99$). The last column settles near 18.3 for $r$ near 1, about $\ln(10^8) = 18.4$ adjusted by the logarithm of the start's ratio $|c_2/c_1|$, as the $1/(1-r)$ law predicts. The bound exceeds the measured counts by 10 to 23% because it assumes that every mode decays at rate $r$. At $r = 1$ the iteration does not distinguish $v_1$ from $v_2$, and it converges to a vector in their plane that depends on the start. Other spectra without a gap produce different failures. I ran each for 2000 steps on a $200 \times 200$ symmetric or orthogonally similar matrix:

- **$\lambda_2 = -\lambda_1$.** The two modes keep equal weight and the second flips sign every step, so $x_k$ alternates between two directions. The error to $v_1$ settled at $\sin\theta = 0.190$ by step 10 and stayed there.
- **A complex pair on top.** A real matrix whose largest eigenvalues are $e^{\pm 0.3i}$ rotates its iterate within a plane at every step. The distance from that plane fell to $10^{-15}$ by step 100, so the iteration converges to the plane but to no single vector in it.
- **A defective top eigenvalue.** With a $2 \times 2$ Jordan block for $\lambda_1 = 1$, the $k\lambda^{k-1}$ term dominates only by a factor of $k$, so the error falls like $1/k$ instead of geometrically. Measured $k \sin\theta_k$ was 1.054, 1.005 and 1.0005 at $k = 100$, 1000 and 10000.

### The eigenvalue converges twice as fast, for symmetric matrices

Power iteration estimates the eigenvalue by the Rayleigh quotient $\rho_k = x_k^T A x_k$ for unit $x_k$. For a symmetric matrix the Rayleigh quotient is stationary at eigenvectors, so an error of order $\theta$ in the vector gives an error of order $\theta^2$ in $\rho$, so the eigenvalue error falls like $r^{2k}$ (the same Theorem 27.1). In the controlled runs above the eigenvalue reached $10^{-8}$ in roughly half the steps the vector needed, 49 against 87.5 at $r = 0.8$. In the figure below, the orange line shows that error.

The squaring of the error requires symmetry. I built non-symmetric matrices $A = V D V^{-1}$ with the same kind of spectrum at $r = 0.8$, tilting $v_2$ toward $v_1$ so the angle between them was 90°, 10°, 1° or 0.1° (eigenvector condition numbers from 1 to about 1150). The Rayleigh quotient's per-step factor went from 0.62 at 90°, close to $r^2 = 0.64$, to 0.80 at every tilted angle, and its median step count went from 43.5 to 76. The direction kept converging at $r$, with factors of 0.79 throughout. Tilting also reduced the direction error, because the remaining component along $v_2$ points almost along $v_1$ and displaces $x_k$ only slightly from that line; median steps fell from 84.5 to 58.

### Real data: principal components

The top eigenvector of a covariance matrix is the first principal component, and the gap is determined by the data. Each dataset was run from ten random starts, stopping at $\sin \theta \le 10^{-8}$.

| Dataset | Shape | $\lambda_2/\lambda_1$ | Steps, median (range) |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| scikit-learn digits | 1797 × 64 | 0.915 | 207.5 (192 to 226) |
| MNIST training images | 60000 × 784 | 0.731 | 60 (49 to 70) |
| Wine, raw features | 178 × 13 | 0.0017 | 3 (3 to 4) |
| Wine, standardized | 178 × 13 | 0.531 | 29.5 (28 to 32) |
| Breast cancer, raw | 569 × 30 | 0.0165 | 5 (4 to 6) |
| Breast cancer, standardized | 569 × 30 | 0.429 | 23 (17 to 24) |

The per-step factor matched $\lambda_2/\lambda_1$ to four digits on every row. The raw wine and breast cancer rows converge in a handful of steps because features with large units account for almost all of the variance. Proline makes up 0.9998 of wine's first component, and the two cell-nucleus area columns dominate that of breast cancer. The first principal component then consists almost entirely of those columns. Standardizing the columns gives a more informative component and a smaller gap, with ratios of 0.531 and 0.429 in place of 0.0017 and 0.0165.

### Real data: PageRank on this site

PageRank is the stationary distribution of a random surfer who follows a link with probability $\alpha$ and jumps to a uniformly random page otherwise ([Page et al., 1999](http://ilpubs.stanford.edu:8090/422/)). It is the top eigenvector, with eigenvalue 1, of the Google matrix $G = \alpha S + (1 - \alpha)\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T$, where $S$ is the row-stochastic link matrix with dangling pages pointed at every page. [Haveliwala and Kamvar](http://ilpubs.stanford.edu:8090/582/) proved $|\lambda_2(G)| \le \alpha$, with equality whenever the link graph has at least two closed sets of pages that no link leaves. The choice of $\alpha$ therefore determines the gap. Power iteration converges at rate $\alpha$ regardless of the link structure, which is a principal reason that it remained the method used for PageRank ([Langville and Meyer, 2004, §5.1](https://doi.org/10.1080/15427951.2004.10129091)).

I built the link graph of this site's notes from their links: 265 notes, 1223 links, 4 dangling notes and 4 closed sets. A dense eigensolver put $|\lambda_2|$ at $\alpha$ to 14 digits for every $\alpha$ I tried. The iteration started from the uniform vector and stopped when a step changed $x$ by less than $10^{-10}$ in the 1-norm.

| $\alpha$ | Iterations | Measured per-step factor |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0.5 | 27 | 0.477 |
| 0.7 | 51 | 0.691 |
| 0.85 | 108 | 0.846 |
| 0.9 | 164 | 0.896 |
| 0.95 | 325 | 0.947 |
| 0.99 | 1314 | 0.987 |

At the $\alpha = 0.85$ that [Brin and Page reported using](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0169-7552(98)00110-X), the iteration requires about a hundred sparse matrix-vector products to reach $10^{-10}$, close to the 142 that Langville and Meyer's estimate $\log_{10}\tau / \log_{10}\alpha$ predicts. At 0.99, where the ranking follows links more closely, it requires twelve times as many. The measured factors are at or slightly below $\alpha$. A plausible explanation, not tested here, is that the slowest eigenvectors are supported on the four small closed sets, to which the uniform start gives little weight.

### Methods for small gaps

Power iteration uses only the most recent vector. Methods that retain more information, or that transform the matrix, are not bound by the $1/(1-r)$ law.

- **Shifts.** Iterating with $A - \sigma I$ changes the ratio to $|\lambda_2 - \sigma| / |\lambda_1 - \sigma|$. A well-chosen shift improves the ratio modestly, and only when the approximate location of the spectrum is known.
- **Inverse iteration and Rayleigh quotient iteration.** Iterating with $(A - \sigma I)^{-1}$ converges to the eigenvalue nearest $\sigma$, at a rate equal to the ratio of its distance from $\sigma$ to the next nearest eigenvalue's distance, so a shift close to an eigenvalue converges in a few steps. Updating the shift to the current Rayleigh quotient every step converges cubically for symmetric matrices, tripling the correct digits per step ([Trefethen and Bau, Theorem 27.3](https://doi.org/10.1137/1.9780898719574)). Each step requires a linear solve.
- **Lanczos.** Lanczos retains every iterate and takes the best approximation in the Krylov space $\mathrm{span}\{x_0, Ax_0, \dots, A^{k-1}x_0\}$. The Kaniel-Paige-Saad bound limits the eigenvalue error to a factor $1/T_{k-1}(1 + 2\gamma)^2$, where $T$ is a Chebyshev polynomial and $\gamma = (\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)/(\lambda_2 - \lambda_n)$ ([Saad, 1980](https://doi.org/10.1137/0717059)). For a small gap that is roughly $(1 + 2\sqrt{\gamma})^{-2k}$, so the step count grows like $1/\sqrt{\text{gap}}$ instead of $1/\text{gap}$.

The Lanczos column is plain Lanczos with full reorthogonalization, and the last is SciPy's `eigsh`:

| $r$ | Power iteration | Lanczos | `eigsh` |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |

At $r = 0.999$ power iteration needs 150 times as many products as Lanczos. [`eigsh`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh.html) wraps ARPACK's implicitly restarted Lanczos method and ran to a tighter tolerance here, which accounts for its extra products. Power iteration remains preferable when memory permits storing only one vector, when the gap is guaranteed (PageRank), or when one step per outer iteration suffices because the vector is reused, as in spectral normalization.

> [!note]- Deep dive: how a random start enters
> The bound starts from $\tan\theta_0$. For a Gaussian random start in $n$ dimensions, $\tan\theta_0$ is about $\sqrt{n}$, so the start adds about $\tfrac{1}{2}\ln n / \ln(1/r)$ steps, a mild dependence on dimension. [Kuczyński and Woźniakowski (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1137/0613066) bound the eigenvalue error for a positive definite matrix and a random start with no gap assumption at all. [Martinsson and Tropp's survey (§6)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.01387) restates the result as an expected relative error of about $\ln n / k$ for power iteration and $(\ln n / k)^2$ for Lanczos after $k$ steps. A small gap slows the convergence of the vector only. The eigenvalue estimate becomes accurate regardless, since a vector that mixes $v_1$ and $v_2$ with $\lambda_2 \approx \lambda_1$ has a Rayleigh quotient close to $\lambda_1$ anyway.

## NumPy and numerical caveats

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[3.0, 1.0], [1.0, 3.0]])
lam, P = np.linalg.eig(A)
print(lam)                                # [4.+0.j 2.+0.j] on NumPy 2.5; [4. 2.] on 2.4 and earlier
print(np.allclose(P @ np.diag(lam) @ np.linalg.inv(P), A))  # True

k = 10  # matrix powers through the eigendecomposition
Ak = P @ np.diag(lam**k) @ np.linalg.inv(P)
print(np.allclose(Ak, np.linalg.matrix_power(A, k)))  # True

w, Q = np.linalg.eigh(A)
print(w)                                  # [2. 4.]
```

The following caveats are taken from the [`numpy.linalg.eig`](https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.linalg.eig.html) and [`eigh`](https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.linalg.eigh.html) documentation and NumPy's source.

- `eig` does not sort eigenvalues. Here it returns 4 before 2, and no order is guaranteed.
- The result's type depends on the NumPy version, not the BLAS. Through NumPy 2.4, `eig` cast its output to real when every imaginary part was exactly zero. NumPy 2.5 always returns complex arrays, though the docstring still describes the cast. Call `.real` after checking the imaginary parts, or use `eigh` when the matrix is symmetric. A real matrix with complex eigenvalues, such as a rotation, gives conjugate pairs in every version.
- For symmetric or Hermitian matrices, use `eigh`. It returns real eigenvalues in ascending order and orthonormal eigenvectors, and it is faster and more accurate because it exploits symmetry.
- For the top few eigenpairs of a large or sparse matrix, use `scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh` (symmetric) or `eigs`, which only need matrix-vector products.
- Nearly defective matrices are ill-conditioned for eigendecomposition. When the eigenvector matrix is close to singular, $P^{-1}$ amplifies error, and the eigenvectors of a non-symmetric matrix can be ill-conditioned even when its eigenvalues are well conditioned. For a non-symmetric matrix, $P D^k P^{-1}$ is not a reliable route to $A^k$, the same reason [Moler and Van Loan](https://doi.org/10.1137/S00361445024180) rate eigenvector methods poorly for the matrix exponential. For a symmetric matrix, the orthogonal $Q$ keeps the reconstruction numerically stable.

> - **Error measure**: $\sin\theta_k = \lVert u - (u^T v_1) v_1 \rVert$ for unit $u = x_k / \lVert x_k \rVert$, which stays accurate down to about $10^{-16}$. The per-step factor is the geometric mean of the error's ratio over the steps where it lies between $10^{-11}$ and $10^{-4}$.
> - **Controlled matrices**: $A = Q\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda)\,Q^T$ with $Q$ from the QR factorization of a Gaussian matrix, $\lambda_1 = 1$, $\lambda_2 = r$, the rest uniform in $[-r, r]$, and Gaussian starts. Seeds 0 to 9 per row. The bound column is the smallest $k$ with $\tan\theta_0 r^k \le 10^{-8}$ for that seed's own start.
> - **Non-symmetric matrices**: $A = V\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda)\,V^{-1}$ where $V$ is orthogonal except that its second column is $\cos\phi\, q_1 + \sin\phi\, q_2$.
> - **Edge cases**: one $200 \times 200$ matrix each, $Q B Q^T$ with $B$ holding the competing eigenvalues (or the rotation block or Jordan block) and the rest uniform in $[-0.5, 0.5]$.
> - **Lanczos**: the textbook three-term recurrence with full reorthogonalization (two Gram-Schmidt passes), checking the top Ritz vector after every step. `eigsh(k=1, which="LA", tol=1e-12)` counted its products through a `LinearOperator`; its final errors were below $10^{-11}$.
> - **PCA**: covariance of the mean-centered data, eigenvalues from `numpy.linalg.eigh`. Standardized means scikit-learn's `StandardScaler`. MNIST is Keras's copy (SHA-256 `731c5ac6…b673d1`) scaled to $[0, 1]$.
> - **PageRank**: every `.md` and `.mdx` file under `notes/content` except templates and archived notes is a page. Edges are its wiki links and Markdown links that resolve to another page, without duplicates or self-links. Dangling pages link to every page, and closed sets are strongly connected components with no edge leaving them. The site had 265 notes on the run date, so the counts change as notes are added.
> - **Environment**: Intel Xeon at 2.10 GHz, 4 cores, Python 3.13.18, NumPy 2.5.3 with OpenBLAS 0.3.34, SciPy 1.18.1. Everything is deterministic given the seeds, and only step counts are reported, no timings.
> - **The figure** runs the same iteration in JavaScript on a $40 \times 40$ matrix with eigenvalues 1, $r$, and 38 more evenly spaced over $[-0.8r, 0.8r]$. Its tests check every step against the closed form $\tan\theta_k = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i \ge 2} (c_i \lambda_i^k)^2} / |c_1|$ and the Rayleigh quotient against $\sum c_i^2 \lambda_i^{2k+1} / \sum c_i^2 \lambda_i^{2k}$.
