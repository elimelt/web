A clock in a distributed system assigns each event a timestamp, and comparisons of timestamps answer ordering questions: which of two conflicting writes is retained, which log entry precedes another, and whether a snapshot is consistent. A physical clock approximates real time with an error that synchronization bounds but does not eliminate, so physical timestamps alone cannot answer these questions. Lamport clocks and vector clocks order events by causality and ignore physical time. Hybrid logical clocks preserve causality while staying close to physical time. TrueTime makes the error of physical time explicit and delays each commit until that error has elapsed.

Lamport's paper, including the mutual exclusion algorithm built on these clocks, is treated in [[systems/distributed-systems/ordering-events-in-distributed-systems|Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events]].

## Physical Clocks

### Drift

A server keeps time by counting the oscillations of a quartz crystal, and no two crystals oscillate at quite the same rate. The [NTP FAQ](https://www.eecis.udel.edu/~ntp/ntpfaq/NTP-s-sw-clocks.htm) puts a typical crystal's sensitivity at about 1 part per million (ppm) per degree Celsius, and 12 ppm of error adds up to roughly a second a day. Huygens measured datacenter servers directly: pairs of clocks ran at rates typically 5 to 10 µs per second apart, and up to 32 µs per second, which is 32 ppm ([Geng et al. 2018](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi18/presentation/geng), Table 1). Without synchronization, two such servers can diverge by 2 ms within a minute.

### Synchronization

The Network Time Protocol (NTP) corrects drift by querying a more accurate reference clock. The client records four timestamps: $T_1$ when its request leaves, $T_2$ when the server receives it, $T_3$ when the server replies, and $T_4$ when the reply arrives. $T_1$ and $T_4$ come from the client's clock, $T_2$ and $T_3$ from the server's. [RFC 5905](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5905) estimates the offset $\theta$ between the two clocks and the round-trip delay $\delta$ as

$$
\theta = \frac{(T_2 - T_1) + (T_3 - T_4)}{2}, \qquad \delta = (T_4 - T_1) - (T_3 - T_2).
$$

The offset estimate assumes that the request and the reply took equally long. An asymmetry between the two cannot be detected from the four timestamps, so the true offset can lie anywhere in $\theta \pm \delta/2$ ([Mills](https://www.eecis.udel.edu/~mills/exec.html)). Synchronization error therefore comes from delay asymmetry, and in a busy network asymmetry comes mostly from queuing. For this reason NTP's clock filter selects its lowest-delay samples, and the more precise systems in the table below timestamp packets in the network card instead of in software.

Reported accuracy ranges from tens of milliseconds over the internet to nanoseconds with hardware support.

| Setup | Reported accuracy | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NTP over the internet | a few tens of ms | [RFC 5905](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5905) |
| NTP on a fast LAN | a few hundred µs | [RFC 5905](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5905) |
| Meta's fleet, ntpd replaced by chrony (2020) | −10 to +3 ms before, ±200 µs after, ±100 µs with NIC timestamps | [Meta](https://engineering.fb.com/2020/03/18/production-engineering/ntp-service/) |
| AWS EC2 (2023) | error bound under 100 µs over NTP, under 40 µs through the hardware clock | [AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/compute/its-about-time-microsecond-accurate-clocks-on-amazon-ec2-instances) |
| Meta's PTP rollout (2022) | under 100 ns of variation over 24 hours | [Meta](https://engineering.fb.com/2022/11/21/production-engineering/precision-time-protocol-at-meta/) |
| Huygens, 40% network load | 11 to 15 ns mean error, 30 to 45 ns at the 99th percentile | [Geng et al. 2018](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi18/presentation/geng) |

The Precision Time Protocol (PTP) reaches nanosecond accuracy with support from the network, in the form of switches that timestamp packets in hardware and account for their own queuing delay. Huygens reaches comparable accuracy without that support. It sends probes in pairs with a known spacing and keeps a pair only if it arrives with the same spacing, since queuing increases the spacing of a pair. Every two seconds it fits each pair of machines' offset and relative drift with a support vector machine over the surviving probes. Then it uses the fact that offsets around any loop of machines must sum to zero to correct the pairwise estimates against each other. On the same 1 Gbps testbed, NTP with hardware timestamps averaged 178 ns of error on an idle network and 78 µs at 40% load, so the error under load came from queuing delay.

### Wall clocks and monotonic clocks

Operating systems expose two kinds of clock. A wall clock (`CLOCK_REALTIME` on Linux) tracks civil time and can jump when synchronization corrects it. A monotonic clock (`CLOCK_MONOTONIC`) only moves forward and is the appropriate clock for measuring a duration on one machine, such as a timeout or a [[systems/distributed-systems/failure-detectors-leases-leader-election|lease]].

Leap seconds introduce a further irregularity in wall clocks. UTC occasionally inserts a 61st second, which software that assumes 60-second minutes must handle. Google avoids the discontinuity by [smearing](https://developers.google.com/time/smear) the extra second over 24 hours, noon to noon, which slows its clocks by about 11.6 ppm, well inside ordinary crystal error. In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures [decided](https://www.bipm.org/en/cgpm-2022/resolution-4) to allow a larger gap between UTC and astronomical time by 2035, so that UTC can stop taking leap seconds.

## Why Timestamps Alone Can't Order Events

Suppose P1 writes `x = 1` and then sends a message to P2, and P2, having read the message, writes `x = 2`. The second write causally follows the first. If the system keeps whichever write has the later timestamp, a policy called last writer wins, it should keep `x = 2`. But if P2's clock is behind P1's by more than the message took to arrive, P2's write carries the *earlier* timestamp and `x = 1` is retained. No error is reported, and the newer value is lost.

Better synchronization narrows this window but does not eliminate it. Any two events stamped closer together than the clocks' error can be ordered either way. A physical timestamp orders events only when a bound on its error is known and the events are further apart than that bound. Spanner's [TrueTime](#truetime-waiting-out-the-uncertainty) is built on this condition. The alternative discards physical time and orders events by potential causal influence.

## Happens-Before

[Lamport (1978)](https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/pubs/time-clocks.pdf) defines the order a system can actually observe. Event $a$ **happens before** event $b$, written $a \to b$, if any of these hold:

1. $a$ and $b$ are on the same process and $a$ comes first.
2. $a$ sends a message and $b$ receives it.
3. $a \to c$ and $c \to b$ for some event $c$.

Sending and receiving are each events in their own right. Two events are **concurrent** if neither happens before the other. Concurrency does not imply simultaneity. It means that no chain of process steps and messages connects the two events, so neither could have influenced the other, and a correct system must not depend on their order. Happens-before is therefore a partial order, under which some pairs of events are unordered.

## Lamport Clocks

Each process keeps a counter $L$ and follows two rules:

- Before every event, including sends and receives, increment $L$. A send carries the new value.
- On receiving a message stamped $L_m$, set $L = \max(L, L_m) + 1$.

This guarantees the **clock condition**: if $a \to b$ then $L(a) < L(b)$. With these rules, $L(e)$ also equals the length of the longest happens-before chain that ends at $e$, counting $e$ itself. The figure's tests check both properties on 300 random diagrams.

The converse fails. $L(a) < L(b)$ says nothing when $a$ and $b$ are concurrent, because each process's counter advances at its own rate. Breaking ties by process id turns Lamport timestamps into a total order that respects causality, which is what [[systems/distributed-systems/mutual-exclusion|Lamport's mutual exclusion]] uses. That total order is arbitrary on concurrent events, and a Lamport timestamp alone cannot determine whether two events were concurrent.

## Vector Clocks

A vector clock replaces the single counter with a vector in order to characterize causality exactly. Fidge (1988) and [Mattern (1989)](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.895/fall02/papers/Mattern/VirtTime_GlobState.pdf) developed it independently. Process $i$ keeps a vector $V$ with one entry per process:

- Before a local event or a send, increment $V[i]$. A send carries a copy of $V$.
- On receiving $V_m$, set $V[j] = \max(V[j], V_m[j])$ for every $j$, then increment $V[i]$.

Entry $j$ of $V(e)$ counts the events on process $j$ that happen before $e$, counting $e$ itself. The comparison rule follows from this interpretation. Define $V(a) < V(b)$ when every entry of $V(a)$ is at most the matching entry of $V(b)$ and at least one is smaller. Then

$$
a \to b \iff V(a) < V(b),
$$

and $a$ and $b$ are concurrent exactly when neither vector is smaller. The figure's tests check this equivalence, and the counting interpretation, for every pair of events in 300 random diagrams.

The cost of this precision is size. A vector clock has an entry per process, and [Charron-Bost (1991)](https://researchr.org/publication/Charron-Bost91) proved that characterizing causality in general requires vectors of that length. The overhead grows in systems with many participants or with participants that join and leave, and the variants in the deep dive below address it. Vector clocks are used to check [[systems/distributed-systems/consistent-global-state|consistent global snapshots]], and causal delivery in [[systems/distributed-systems/crdts|operation-based CRDTs]] relies on them.

```java
public class VectorClock {
  private final int[] clock;
  private final int id;

  public VectorClock(int id, int n) {
    this.id = id;
    this.clock = new int[n];
  }

  // A local event.
  public void tick() {
    clock[id]++;
  }

  // A send: tick, then attach a copy of the clock to the message.
  public int[] send() {
    tick();
    return clock.clone();
  }

  // A receive: merge the sender's clock, then tick.
  public void receive(int[] senderClock) {
    for (int j = 0; j < clock.length; j++)
      clock[j] = Math.max(clock[j], senderClock[j]);
    tick();
  }

  // True iff the event stamped `a` happens before the event stamped `b`.
  public static boolean happenedBefore(int[] a, int[] b) {
    boolean strictlyLess = false;
    for (int j = 0; j < a.length; j++) {
      if (a[j] > b[j]) return false;
      if (a[j] < b[j]) strictlyLess = true;
    }
    return strictlyLess;
  }
}
```

Two events are concurrent exactly when `happenedBefore(a, b)` and `happenedBefore(b, a)` are both false.

> [!note]- Deep dive: version vectors, dotted version vectors, and interval tree clocks
> A replicated store needs to determine whether one *version* of an object supersedes another; the relation between every pair of events is not required. A **version vector** has an entry per replica that has written the object and is compared exactly like a vector clock. [[systems/distributed-systems/dynamo-db|Dynamo]] stored one per object, as (node, counter) pairs, and returned concurrent versions to the application to merge. To keep the vectors small it dropped the oldest pairs past a threshold, which can make a newer version look concurrent with an older one.
>
> A subtler problem arises when clients write through a server. If the server increments its own entry for each client write, two clients' concurrent writes can receive vectors in which one appears newer, and one write is lost without any error. An entry per client avoids this but makes vectors grow with the number of clients. [Dotted version vectors](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.5808) keep one entry per server and tag each write with a single extra (server, counter) pair, its *dot*, which is sufficient to distinguish concurrent writes. Riak added them in version 2.0, and its [documentation](https://docs.riak.com/riak/kv/2.2.3/learn/concepts/causal-context) calls them far better at limiting the number of siblings.
>
> Vector clocks also assume a fixed set of process ids. [Interval tree clocks](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-92221-6_18) (Almeida, Baquero, and Fonte, 2008) let processes fork and join without a global id registry by giving each one a share of the interval $[0, 1)$ to count in.
>
> Git takes the opposite approach. A commit graph stores the whole causal history explicitly, and `git merge-base --is-ancestor` answers happens-before by searching it. A vector clock is a compressed summary of that same history that can answer the question without the graph.

## Hybrid Logical Clocks

Logical timestamps carry no information about physical time. A Lamport or vector timestamp says nothing about when an event happened, so it cannot support a query for "the state as of 10:00" or the expiry of data after an hour. [Hybrid logical clocks](https://www.cse.buffalo.edu//~demirbas/publications/hlc.pdf) (HLC, Kulkarni et al. 2014) combine the two. Each process keeps a pair $(l, c)$: $l$ is the largest physical time it has observed, and $c$ is a counter that orders events sharing the same $l$. With $pt$ the process's physical clock reading:

- Before a local event or a send: $l' = l$, then $l = \max(l', pt)$. If $l$ did not change, increment $c$; otherwise set $c = 0$.
- On receiving $(l_m, c_m)$: $l' = l$, then $l = \max(l', l_m, pt)$. Set $c$ to $\max(c, c_m) + 1$ if $l$ equals both $l'$ and $l_m$, to $c + 1$ if it equals only $l'$, to $c_m + 1$ if it equals only $l_m$, and to 0 if $pt$ is the maximum.

Timestamps compare by $l$, then $c$. HLC satisfies the same clock condition as Lamport clocks: if $a \to b$, then $(l, c)$ of $a$ is smaller. The paper also proves $l \ge pt$ always and $l - pt \le \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon$ bounds how far apart the physical clocks are. $l$ therefore stays within the synchronization error of real time, which allows an HLC to fit in the 64 bits of an NTP timestamp, with 48 bits of $l$ at microsecond resolution and 16 bits of $c$. [CockroachDB](https://www.cockroachlabs.com/blog/clock-management-cockroachdb/) and [MongoDB](https://researchr.org/publication/TyulenevSKTCM19) both timestamp transactions with HLCs.

In the figure below, P2's clock runs 9 ms slow. P1 sends at physical time 112 and P2 receives at its own 106, so ordering by physical timestamps places the receive before the send.

### How large the counter gets

The paper proves a bound on $c$ that grows with the number of processes and the clock spread, which is too loose to establish that a 16-bit field suffices. In the paper's measurements the largest $c$ was 3 on four nodes and 9 on an 8-node AWS cluster. Those runs do not show how $c$ behaves at higher event rates, so I simulated it. Eight processes run the HLC rules above with microsecond clocks, half of all events send a message to a random process, and one-way delays are lognormal with a 100 µs median. Clock offsets are spread over $\varepsilon$, and each configuration ran with 5 seeds.

| Clock spread $\varepsilon$ | Events/s per process | $c$, 99th percentile | Largest $c$ | Largest $l - pt$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0.1 ms | 10,000 | 1 ± 0 | 5 ± 1 | 58 µs |
| 0.1 ms | 1,000,000 | 40 ± 13 | 96 ± 18 | 58 µs |
| 1 ms | 10,000 | 9 ± 2 | 22 ± 3 | 0.75 ms |
| 1 ms | 1,000,000 | 115 ± 9 | 199 ± 17 | 0.75 ms |
| 10 ms | 10,000 | 24 ± 3 | 47 ± 5 | 7.6 ms |
| 10 ms | 1,000,000 | 123 ± 1 | 210 ± 12 | 7.6 ms |
| 1 ms, plus one clock 100 ms ahead | 1,000,000 | 123 ± 2 | 213 ± 22 | 100.3 ms |

Values are means ± standard deviation over seeds; the [full table](clocks-experiments/results.txt) has all 12 configurations.

- **The counter stays small.** Even at a million events per second per process, the largest $c$ in any configuration averaged about 210, three thousandths of the 65,535 a 16-bit field holds. It grows with the event rate and hardly at all with clock spread past 1 ms. A process's counter resets whenever a larger $l$ reaches it, and the fastest clock in the system produces a larger $l$ every microsecond.
- **Hybrid time follows the fastest clock.** The largest $l - pt$ equals the actual spread of the clocks, so slow processes end up stamping events with the fastest process's time. One clock running 100 ms ahead pushed every process's hybrid time 100 ms ahead of its own clock. HLC still preserves causality, but its timestamps no longer approximate the current physical time.

For this reason CockroachDB bounds clock offset. Its `--max-offset` [defaults to 500 ms](https://docs.cockroachlabs.com/docs/stable/cockroach-start), and a node [shuts itself down](https://docs.cockroachlabs.com/docs/stable/critical-log-messages.md) if its clock is off by more than 80% of that from at least half the cluster.

> [!note]- Measurement record
> - **Code**: [`hlc_counter.py`](clocks-experiments/hlc_counter.py), a discrete-event simulation using only the Python standard library, run as `python3 hlc_counter.py`. Output in [`results.txt`](clocks-experiments/results.txt). It simulates the algorithm; it does not measure a cluster.
> - **Model**: 8 processes; Poisson event arrivals at the listed rate per process; each event sends to a uniformly random other process with probability 0.5; one-way delay lognormal with median 100 µs and $\sigma = 0.5$; physical clocks read whole microseconds; each clock has a fixed offset drawn uniformly from $[-\varepsilon/2, \varepsilon/2]$, so the realized spread is somewhat under $\varepsilon$. Drift is left out: at the 32 µs per second Huygens measured, it moves a clock by at most a few microseconds over a run.
> - **Runs**: 400,000 events per run, the first 10% of simulated time discarded as warmup, 5 seeds per configuration, 12 configurations. The whole sweep took 48 s on one core of an Intel Xeon at 2.10 GHz with Python 3.13.18.
> - **Not modeled**: drift, clock steps, bursts, message loss, and processes that communicate with only a few peers. A process that rarely receives timestamps derived from the fastest clock keeps incrementing $c$ in between, so sparse communication probably raises the counter.

> [!note]- Deep dive: how CockroachDB reads with uncertain clocks
> CockroachDB serves reads at an HLC timestamp. A value written by another node with a slightly later timestamp might have been committed before the read started, in real time, if that node's clock runs ahead. So a read treats any value whose timestamp falls between its own timestamp and its timestamp plus the maximum clock offset as *uncertain*, since the read cannot determine whether the write happened before it. On encountering such a value, the read restarts at a higher timestamp ([Cockroach Labs](https://www.cockroachlabs.com/blog/clock-management-cockroachdb/)). A larger maximum offset tolerates worse clocks and causes more of these restarts, so the setting trades performance against safety.

## TrueTime: Waiting Out the Uncertainty

Spanner uses physical time directly, with an explicit bound on its error. Its TrueTime API returns an interval, `TT.now() = [earliest, latest]`, guaranteed to contain true time ([Corbett et al. 2012](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/osdi12/osdi12-final-16.pdf)). Most of Google's time masters have GPS receivers and the rest have atomic clocks. Each server polls them every 30 seconds and in between assumes its own crystal drifts at a worst-case 200 µs per second. That assumption alone grows the uncertainty by 6 ms between polls, so $\varepsilon$, half the interval's width, follows a sawtooth between about 1 and 7 ms and averages about 4 ms.

To commit a transaction, Spanner picks a timestamp $s$ no earlier than `TT.now().latest`, then waits until `TT.now().earliest` has passed $s$ before making the commit visible. This **commit wait** lasts at least about $2\varepsilon$. After it, $s$ is certainly in the past, so any transaction that starts later in real time gets a larger timestamp. Timestamp order then matches real-time order, the property Spanner calls external consistency (see [[systems/distributed-systems/consistency|consistency]]), and it is obtained without any exchange of messages.

The cost is latency proportional to clock uncertainty, which motivates the synchronization work in the table above. AWS now exposes the same kind of interval through [ClockBound](https://github.com/aws/clock-bound), which returns an (earliest, latest) pair, with an error bound the [EC2 time service](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/compute/its-about-time-microsecond-accurate-clocks-on-amazon-ec2-instances) keeps under 40 µs on supported instances. [Aurora DSQL](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/database/everything-you-dont-need-to-know-about-amazon-aurora-dsql-part-5-how-the-service-uses-clocks) uses it to pick transaction timestamps and waits until a commit timestamp is verifiably in the past. With a 40 µs bound, the wait that took about 8 ms in Spanner in 2012 falls below 100 µs.

## Choosing a Clock

| Requirement | Use | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measure a duration on one machine | A monotonic clock | Nothing |
| Totally order events consistently with causality | Lamport clock with a process-id tiebreak | One integer per message |
| Tell concurrent events from ordered ones | Vector clock or version vector | One entry per process or replica |
| Causal order plus timestamps near real time | Hybrid logical clock | 64 bits; needs a bound on clock offset |
| Real-time order across machines without messages | TrueTime-style intervals with commit wait | Waiting about twice the clock uncertainty |
